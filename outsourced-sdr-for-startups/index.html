What an outsourced SDR does for a startup

A sales development rep (SDR) has one job: start sales conversations with the right buyers and pass the good ones to someone who can close. At a startup that someone is usually a founder or one early account executive. An outsourced SDR does the same job from outside your payroll.

In practice the work breaks into a few parts:

  • Defining who to contact: the titles, company types and problems that fit your offer
  • Building and cleaning a list of real contacts at those companies
  • Writing the first message and the follow-ups
  • Sending through email, LinkedIn and phone
  • Reading every reply and answering it
  • Booking the meeting on your calendar with the context your seller needs

Nothing here is new. Every sales team does these steps. The difference is who owns the daily effort. With an outsourced provider, the setup, the sending and the reply handling sit outside your company, so a founder is not splitting the week between product work and prospecting.

Startup buyers sometimes call this an outsourced sales team for startups. The label varies. What matters is the scope. A good provider handles the top of the funnel and stops at the meeting. Your team runs discovery, demos and the close.

Outsourced SDR vs appointment setting vs outsourced closing: which job do you need?

Three labels get mixed up when founders shop for outbound help. Each one covers a different slice of the sales process. Knowing which slice you need keeps you from buying too little or too much.

Outsourced SDR work. This covers the whole front end of outbound. The provider helps define the targets, builds the list, writes the messages, sends them across channels, reads the replies and books the meeting. The scope runs from the first research step to a call on the calendar.

Appointment setting. This is a narrower job. The focus is the meeting itself: reaching a buyer, confirming interest and getting a time agreed. Some providers in this category work from a list you supply. Others cover more of the front end. The label alone does not tell you, so ask where the scope starts and where it stops.

Outsourced closing. Here an outside party runs the sales calls and tries to win the deal for you. It is a much bigger step. The buyer hears from someone who is not on your team at the moment that matters most. For a young company, that is a risky place to step back.

Here is a simple way to match the job to your situation:

  • You have a founder or seller who can take meetings and want someone to fill the calendar: outsourced SDR work fits
  • You already have a good list and tested messages but nobody has time to follow up and book: appointment setting may be enough
  • Nobody on your side can run sales calls and you want someone else to talk to buyers: that is a closing handoff, so think hard before you do it

Most early startups should keep the close in house. The first calls with buyers teach you how to sell. They show you which objections come up and which features matter. If an outside party holds those conversations, that learning leaves with them.

Whichever job you buy, ask the provider three plain questions. Which of these jobs are you actually selling me? Who owns each step from first message to signed deal? Who talks to the buyer after the meeting is booked?

Write the answers down. A clear line between the provider's job and yours saves arguments later. It also helps your seller walk into each call knowing what the buyer has already heard.

Is your startup ready for outsourced SDR work?

Outsourced outbound pays off when some groundwork is already in place. If they are not, the provider will spend its time guessing. You will pay for the guessing.

Check these before you talk to anyone:

  • You can name the buyer in one sentence: a title, a type of company and a problem
  • You have an offer you can explain in a short message without a demo
  • Someone on your side can take meetings and follow up quickly
  • You have at least a few real conversations with buyers behind you, even if they did not all close
  • You can tell a good meeting from a bad one. You can write that down

The last point matters most. A provider can only book what you define. If "qualified" means "anyone who replied," you will get a full calendar and an empty pipeline. If it means "a person with this title at this kind of company who has this problem," you will get something your seller can use.

You do not need a polished sales process, a full CRM setup or a sales hire. You do need a clear buyer, a clear offer and a person to take the call. Those three are the real entry ticket.

Before product-market fit: when outsourcing helps and when it does not

Many founders ask whether they should outsource outbound before they have found product-market fit. The honest answer is that it depends on what you are trying to learn.

Before product-market fit, your offer and your buyer are still moving. Outbound can help in that stage if you treat it as research. A focused campaign to one type of buyer shows you who replies, what they say and which problem gets attention. A provider that sends the work out and shares the replies can speed up that learning.

It does not help if you expect outbound to prove the product for you. A message cannot rescue an offer nobody wants. If the pitch changes every few days, the provider cannot test anything, because the test keeps changing under it.

Outsourcing before fit makes sense when:

  • You have one or two buyer types you want to test
  • Founders are too busy building to prospect with any consistency
  • You want clean data on who responds before you hire anyone
  • You are willing to read the replies yourself and adjust the offer

It is a poor choice when:

  • You have no idea who the buyer is
  • The product is not ready for a real customer to try
  • You want the provider to decide your positioning
  • Nobody will be free to take the meetings that come in

Think of early outbound as a structured experiment. The provider runs the experiment. You read the results and decide what to change.

After product-market fit: scaling outbound without a full department

Once buyers are coming back, referring others and closing on a repeatable pitch, the problem changes. You no longer need to learn who the buyer is. You need more of the right conversations than founder networks and inbound can supply.

This is where outsourced outbound often fits best. You know the buyer. You know what a good meeting looks like. The work is to put that definition in front of more people, consistently.

Hiring a first SDR at this stage is a real option, but it asks a lot of a small company. Someone has to recruit, onboard, coach and manage the rep. Someone has to choose the sending tools, set up the domains and keep email deliverability healthy. If the rep leaves or does not work out, the company starts over. A provider already has the tooling and the routine in place.

Outsourcing after fit also gives you a clean way to grow. You can run outbound while you decide what the in-house version should look like. Some companies keep the provider long term. Others use it to prove a motion and then bring part of it in-house. Either path is fine. The point is that you do not have to choose between building a department and doing nothing.

Handing off founder-led sales without losing what you learned

Most startups begin with founder-led sales. The founder writes the emails, takes the calls and learns the objections firsthand. That knowledge is valuable and it lives in one person's head. A handoff is where it gets lost.

To keep it, turn what you know into something a provider can use. Do this before the first message goes out.

  • The buyer: Write down the titles, company types and triggers that led to your best conversations
  • The pain: Describe the problem in the words buyers used, not the words in your pitch deck
  • The objections: List the pushback you hear most and how you answer it
  • The wins: Note what made a good first call good, so the provider knows what to aim for
  • The no-gos: Name the buyers you do not want, so the list stays clean

Then stay close for the first stretch. Read the early replies. Tell the provider which ones were right and which were off. That feedback is how outbound gets sharper.

A founder does not have to disappear from sales. The goal is to move the repetitive part away from the founder while keeping the founder's judgment in the loop. Founders still join the early meetings. They still hear the buyer directly. They just stop spending their mornings on list building and follow-ups.

A first outbound motion with a small team

A small team does not need a large outbound program. It needs a focused one. A first motion has four moving parts. Each should be simple.

One buyer. Pick a single audience for the first run. A narrow audience makes the message sharper and the results easier to read. Adding a second audience later is easy. Untangling a muddled first run is not.

One offer. Decide what you are asking the buyer to do. Usually it is a short conversation about a specific problem. Keep the ask small and the reason clear.

A few channels. Email is the usual starting point because it scales and is easy to measure. LinkedIn outreach and cold calling can support it when the buyer is active on those channels or hard to reach by email alone. Running them together lets each one reinforce the others.

One clear handoff. Decide who takes the meeting, what happens in the call and how follow-up works. A meeting that lands with nobody ready for it is wasted effort.

A small team also needs a way to review. Set a regular time to read replies together and ask what the market is telling you. Outbound gives you a steady feed of buyer reactions. A startup that reads them and adjusts will learn faster than one that only counts meetings.

Choosing your first channel mix: email, LinkedIn and calling

A first outbound program does not need every channel. It needs the right ones for your buyer. The choice depends on where your buyers pay attention, how many of them exist and how much effort each contact deserves.

Email. Email is the usual starting point. It reaches many people at a reasonable effort per contact and it is easy to measure. It also needs careful setup, because sending reputation affects whether your messages reach the inbox at all. Treat the setup as part of the work, not an extra.

LinkedIn. LinkedIn works well when your buyers have visible roles and use the platform. It tends to be slower per contact, and messages that read like a pitch get ignored fast. It also ties the outreach to a real profile, so the person behind that profile matters. Ask who controls the account and what the messages will say in that person's name.

Calling. Phone outreach suits high-value accounts and buyers who ignore email. It takes more effort per contact, so it fits a short, well-chosen list better than a broad one. Calling works best as support for another channel, not as the only way in.

Three questions help you choose:

  • Where do your best buyers already spend time and respond?
  • How large is your market? A small market calls for fewer, more careful touches. A large one gives you room to test
  • How senior are the buyers? Senior people often respond to a short, direct note or a call more than to a long sequence

A common first mix is email as the base with LinkedIn alongside it. Calling joins for the accounts that matter most. That keeps the program simple enough to read. If you start with all three at once and replies come in, you will not know which channel earned them.

Keep the channels consistent. Your email copy, your LinkedIn note and your call script should sound like one company making one offer. A buyer who hears three different stories will trust none of them.

Add a channel when you have a reason, not because the provider offers it. Good reasons include a buyer type that does not answer email or a set of top accounts that deserve extra attention. Review the mix after the first replies come in and drop what is not helping.

What founders give the provider

A provider can do a lot, but it cannot invent what only you know. The better your inputs, the better the output. Here is what most founders hand over.

  • Your ideal customer profile: The titles, company types, industries and triggers that point to a good fit
  • Your offer: A plain description of what you sell, who it helps and why a buyer would care
  • Proof you can use: Real results, customer names or quotes that you have permission to share, if you have them. If you have none yet, say so. Honest and specific beats inflated
  • Calendar access: A booking link or shared calendar so meetings land where your seller will see them
  • Meeting rules: Who takes the call, what a qualified meeting looks like and what you want to know before the call
  • Exclusions: Current customers, open deals, competitors and anyone you do not want contacted
  • A point of contact: One person who answers questions and approves messages quickly
  • Sending permissions: Access or approvals the provider needs to run outreach under your brand

Speed matters here. A provider that waits days for an answer loses momentum. So does your campaign. Pick one decision maker and give that person the time to respond.

You do not need to write the messages. A good provider drafts them. You review and approve. Your job is to make sure they sound like a company you would want to buy from.

What makes a meeting qualified for a startup, and how to handle no-shows

Every outbound program lives or dies on one definition: what counts as a good meeting. If you leave it vague, the provider will fill in the blanks. You may not like the result.

A useful definition for a startup has four parts:

  • The right person. A named role with the authority to care about the problem or to bring in the people who do
  • The right company. The type, industry and rough size you can serve well today, plus anything that rules a company out
  • A real problem. Some sign that the buyer faces the issue you solve, even if it is small
  • Agreed interest. The person said yes to a call, knows who it is with and knows what it covers

Write these down in plain words and share them before the first message goes out. Review them after the first meetings and tighten them where they proved too loose.

Be clear about what does not qualify. Current customers, competitors, vendors with a pitch of their own, students and people with no role in the decision all fill calendars without helping your pipeline. A reply that says "send me information" is not a meeting. A polite "maybe later" is not a meeting either.

Then deal with no-shows. Some booked meetings will not happen. Agree on the rules ahead of time so nobody argues later:

  • Who sends the confirmation and the reminder before the call
  • Who reaches out to reschedule and how many tries are made
  • Whether a no-show counts as a meeting for reporting and billing
  • How to handle a buyer who reschedules again and again

Give the provider context on your side as well. If your seller is slow to confirm times, buyers lose interest. If calendar slots are rare, meetings drift. A qualified meeting needs a prepared seller as much as it needs a good prospect.

Before each call, ask for a short note that covers who the buyer is, what they said and why they agreed to talk. That note turns a booked slot into a real conversation. After each call, tell the provider whether the meeting was good, fine or off. That feedback is the cheapest way to improve the next batch.

Do not ask a provider to promise a number of meetings. Ask how they will help you learn from the ones that happen.

Who owns the accounts, lists and sending setup in an outsourced program?

Ownership is the topic founders forget until an agreement ends. Settle it at the start, when everyone is on good terms. It takes a short conversation then and a stressful one later.

Several things are in play, and each one can have a different owner:

  • Target accounts. The companies you want to reach
  • The contact list. The people at those companies, with their details and status
  • Sending setup. The domains, mailboxes and tools that send your outreach
  • Conversation history. Every reply, note and booked meeting record
  • Opt-out records. The list of people who asked not to be contacted again
  • Your own profiles. Any LinkedIn account or mailbox that carries a person's name

Who picks the accounts. You should have the final say. A good provider suggests targets based on your buyer definition and shows you the list before sending. You approve it and add exclusions. That keeps outreach away from current customers, open deals and partners.

Who owns the list. Ask this directly. Some providers treat the list as part of their service. Others hand over the file when the work ends. Data from outside sources may also come with usage limits. Ask how the data is sourced and what you can keep.

Who owns the sending setup. Many programs send from separate domains so the main company domain stays clear. Ask who registers those domains and who holds the login. Ask whether the setup can move to you or to a new provider later.

Who holds the history. Replies and notes should be visible to you while the work runs. A shared inbox or your own CRM works well. If the history lives only in the provider's tools, you lose the record of what buyers said when you leave.

Opt-outs. People who asked to stop must stay stopped, whoever sends the next message. Make sure the opt-out record goes with you.

Your profiles. If outreach goes out under a founder's LinkedIn profile or a team member's mailbox, that account stays with the person. Agree on how access is granted and removed.

Put each answer in the agreement, not only in a call. If a provider will not say who owns what, treat that as a warning sign.

Outsourced vs in-house: how the two compare

Neither option is better in every case. They suit different stages and different constraints. Here is a plain comparison.

Speed to start. An outsourced provider already has tools, process and a routine. You can begin once the inputs are agreed. An in-house hire starts with recruiting, then onboarding and training. Sending comes last.

Management load. An in-house rep needs coaching, feedback and goals from someone with the time to give them. In a startup that someone is often a founder. A provider needs less daily management but does need clear direction and quick approvals.

Control. In-house gives you the most direct control. The rep sits in your meetings, hears your product team and absorbs your culture. An outsourced provider works from the brief you write, so the quality of your brief sets the ceiling.

Product knowledge. An in-house rep can learn your product deeply over time. A provider learns what you teach it. For simple offers that is plenty. For very technical ones you will need to invest more in the handoff.

Flexibility. Outsourcing is easier to scale up, pause or end. A hire is a longer commitment.

Learning. Both can produce useful market feedback. What matters is whether someone reads it and acts on it.

Tooling. An in-house program needs sending tools, data sources and email infrastructure that someone has to run. A provider brings its own.

Many startups start outside and bring a version of the work in-house later. If you want a wider view of what a managed program can cover, see what a full managed outbound engagement includes. For a plain look at the decision itself, read whether to hire an SDR or outsource.

Human SDRs vs AI SDR tools: how to weigh each option for a startup

Software that writes and sends outbound messages has become a real option. Founders now ask whether a tool can do the job of a person and whether it is a better fit for a small company. The honest answer depends on what you need done and how much judgment the work requires.

An AI SDR tool is software that handles some or all of the prospecting steps: finding contacts, drafting messages, sending them and sorting replies. A person-led provider uses people for the steps that need judgment and may use software for the rest. Many programs mix the two.

Software tends to be strong at repeatable work. It drafts text quickly, applies the same rules every time and does not tire. That suits tasks like formatting lists, sorting replies into groups and scheduling follow-ups.

Judgment is harder to automate. Someone has to decide whether a reply shows real interest or polite brush-off. Someone has to notice when a target company just changed direction or when a message sounds off for a given buyer. Someone has to decide when not to send. A small company with a narrow market and a pitch still taking shape usually has more of these decisions to make.

Here are questions to ask about any approach, tool or provider:

  • Who reads the messages before they go out?
  • What happens when a buyer replies with a question the system did not expect?
  • How is your sending reputation protected?
  • Who is accountable when a message goes wrong?
  • How are opt-outs recorded and respected?
  • Can you see everything that was sent in your name?
  • What does the work look like when something breaks?

A tool you run yourself also needs a person on your side. Someone has to set it up, feed it good inputs, check the output and handle the replies. For a startup short on time, that can erase the savings.

Think about risk as well. Messages go out under your brand. A poor message sent at scale reaches many buyers before anyone notices. Review matters more as volume grows.

So the real question is not human or AI. It is who checks the work and who answers for it. Pick the setup where you can see what is sent, correct it quickly and learn from the replies.

What drives the cost of each option

Cost is the question founders ask first. Because every program is different, this page explains what moves the cost rather than quoting figures. Ask each provider for a clear written scope so you can compare like with like.

For an in-house hire, the cost comes from:

  • Pay, benefits and payroll taxes for the role
  • Recruiting time or agency fees
  • Sending tools, data subscriptions and email infrastructure
  • The founder or manager time spent on coaching and review
  • The period before a new rep is fully productive
  • The cost of replacing the rep if the role does not work out

For an outsourced provider, the cost comes from:

  • The number of channels in the program: email only versus email with LinkedIn and calling
  • How many buyers the program targets and how many accounts it works
  • How much list building and research each contact needs
  • How much reply handling and meeting booking the provider does
  • The length and structure of the agreement

Some of these costs are easy to see. Others are hidden. Founder time is the one startups most often forget. An hour a founder spends managing outbound is an hour not spent on product or customers. When you compare options, count that time.

Be careful with any provider that leads with a low fee and leaves the scope vague. A cheap program that sends little or sends to the wrong people costs more in the end than a clear one. Ask what is included, what is extra and what happens if the first approach needs to change.

How outsourced SDR providers charge: retainer, per meeting and hybrid

Providers use a few common pricing models. Each one shifts risk between you and the provider in a different way. This section explains the models in words only. Figures vary too much by scope to be useful, so ask each provider to price the same defined program.

Retainer. You pay a regular fee for a defined scope of work. The fee covers the effort, not a set outcome. The upside is predictability: you know what you pay each period and the provider can plan its work. The downside is that you pay even in a slow stretch. What to watch is scope. Make sure the channels, the audiences and the reporting are spelled out.

Per meeting. The fee is due once a meeting is booked and accepted. This ties the fee to something you can see. It sounds simple, but the details decide whether it works. Ask what counts as a billable meeting, how duplicates and no-shows are handled and who judges whether a meeting fits your definition. A model that pays only for volume can reward a full calendar over a useful one.

Hybrid. You pay a base fee plus a variable part tied to meetings or another agreed result. It balances the two. The provider has steady funding to do the setup work and a reason to care about outcomes. The cost is complexity, so make sure the math is written clearly.

Dedicated or shared capacity. Some providers charge for a set amount of a person's time. Others charge for a program scope that many clients share. Ask which one you are buying and what happens if the person assigned to you changes.

Also ask about the pieces around the fee:

  • Whether there is a setup charge and what it covers
  • Which channels are included and which are extra
  • Whether data, tools and sending infrastructure are part of the fee
  • How long the agreement runs and how to change scope
  • What happens to the fee if you pause the work

When you compare offers, picture two situations: a slow month and a busy month. Work out what you would pay in each and what you would get. Count your own time too. A model that looks cheap but needs daily founder attention may cost more than one that runs on its own.

No model is best for everyone. A new startup testing one audience often values a clear scope and a short, flexible agreement. A team that wants to link spend to results may lean toward per-meeting or hybrid terms. Choose the one you can explain to your co-founder in a minute.

What the first weeks involve

A good start is more about setup than sending. Here is the usual order of work. Exact timing depends on your offer, your market and how fast inputs arrive, so no provider should promise a date for results.

Setup. The provider agrees the buyer definition and the meeting rules with you. It prepares the sending infrastructure so your main company domain does not carry cold email volume. It also connects to the calendar where meetings will land.

List. The provider builds a list of contacts that match your definition and cleans it. A strong list is the base of the whole program. Weak data produces weak results however good the writing is. For more on this step, see how to build a B2B lead list.

Messaging. The provider drafts the first message and the follow-ups. You review them and approve. This is where your handoff notes pay off, because the best messages use the buyer's own words.

First conversations. Sending begins in careful steps. Replies come in. Some are interested, some are not and some ask questions. The provider answers. Good matches move toward a booked meeting. You get the context before each call.

Review and adjustment. Early replies show what to change. Maybe the title is wrong, the pain is off or the offer needs a sharper line. A good provider shares what it sees and adjusts with you.

Treat the first stretch as calibration. The aim is to learn what works for your buyer so the program can improve from there.

The first 90 days with an outsourced SDR provider: phase by phase

People often talk about the first 90 days as a planning window. This section lays out the phases of work in order and what you should be doing in each one. It does not say when results will arrive. Timing depends on your market, your offer and how quickly decisions are made on both sides.

Phase one: alignment. The goal is a shared picture of the buyer. Work through your ideal customer in detail. Sort target companies into tiers by fit. Agree on exclusions and on how meetings will be defined. Decide who approves messages and how fast. Your job here is to be available and specific.

Phase two: build and prepare. The provider readies the machinery. Sending domains and mailboxes are set up and brought into use gradually so they build a good reputation. The list gets checked for accuracy and duplicates. Message drafts are written in more than one version so there is something to compare. You review the drafts for tone and truth. Anything you would not say on a call should not appear in a message.

Phase three: controlled launch. Sending starts small and grows as the signals look healthy. The provider watches whether messages reach inboxes and whether replies sound right. Replies get sorted into groups: interested, not now, wrong person, referral and opt-out. Your side needs to answer fast. A warm reply that sits for days cools off.

Phase four: learn and tighten. Now there is real data. Compare audiences, offers and message versions. Change one thing at a time so you know what caused a difference. Review the meetings that happened with your seller. Update the definition of a good meeting if the early ones showed gaps.

Phase five: decide what comes next. At this point you have enough information to choose. You might widen the audience, add a channel, adjust the offer, hold steady or stop. A good provider helps frame the choice with what it saw instead of pushing you toward more spend.

At the end of each phase, hold a short checkpoint. Ask what was done, what was learned, what is blocked and what needs your decision. Write the answers down.

No one can tell you in advance how many meetings a program will book or how fast. Be wary of anyone who does. What a provider can show you is a clear process and honest reporting at every step.

How to review outsourced outbound each week: what your provider should show you

A program you cannot see is a program you cannot improve. Regular reviews keep the work honest on both sides. They also turn a stream of replies into lessons for the whole company.

A weekly review does not need to be long. Keep it short, keep it regular and give it a fixed agenda. Ask your provider to bring the following:

  • What was sent. Messages by channel and audience, so you know what buyers saw
  • Replies by type. Interested, not now, wrong person, referral, question and opt-out
  • Meetings booked and held. Plus no-shows and reschedules
  • Fit of the meetings. How well each one matched your definition
  • Sending health. Signs that messages are reaching inboxes and that the setup is in good shape
  • What the market is saying. Objections, phrases and surprises from real replies
  • The next test. One change planned for the coming week and the reason for it

Read some replies yourself. Summaries are useful, but the buyer's own words show things a summary hides. A founder who reads a few real replies each week stays close to the market, which is one of the biggest benefits of outbound.

Do not judge the program on one number. A high count of replies means little if they are all rejections. A full calendar means little if the meetings are poor. Look at the whole chain from message to reply to meeting to opportunity, and ask where it breaks.

Once a month, take a wider look. Ask whether the audience still makes sense, whether the offer needs a sharper angle and whether the channel mix is right. Ask whether your own side is keeping up with follow-up and meeting prep. Often the weak link is on the buyer's side of the table or on yours, not on the provider's.

Keep a simple log of changes and what happened after each one. After a few weeks you will have a record of what you tried and what you learned. That record is worth having whether you keep the provider, switch or bring the work in house.

How to choose a provider

Many companies offer outbound help. Their pitches sound alike. Look past the pitch and ask questions that show how they work.

  • Ask what is included. Which channels, how many contacts, who writes the messages and who handles replies. Get it in writing
  • Ask how they define a qualified meeting. The answer should match the definition you wrote, not a generic one
  • Ask how they protect your domain. Cold email needs separate sending infrastructure and careful setup
  • Ask how list building works. You want to know where data comes from and how it is checked
  • Ask what you will see. You should have access to the messages, the replies and the meeting details
  • Ask about the agreement. Look at the length, how to change scope and how to end it
  • Ask who you will talk to. You want a clear contact who understands your market
  • Ask for relevant experience. A provider that has worked with buyers like yours will need less ramp-up

Reference calls are useful. Talk to a company that has used the provider and ask what went well and what they would change. A confident provider will usually welcome the request.

Pay attention to how the provider handles your questions on the first call. A provider who explains the trade-offs and admits what depends on your market is easier to trust than one who promises everything.

Red flags when you compare providers

Some warning signs show up early. None is proof of a bad provider on its own, but several together are worth taking seriously.

  • Promised results. Nobody can know your meeting count before the market has seen your offer. Be cautious of firm promises about outcomes
  • Vague scope. If you cannot tell what you are buying, you cannot tell whether you got it
  • No questions about your buyer. A provider that does not ask who you sell to is not planning a targeted program
  • Generic messages. Templates that could be sent by any company to any buyer rarely earn replies
  • Hidden sending practices. If the provider will not explain how it protects your domain, assume the risk is on you
  • Pressure to sign fast. A good fit holds up under a few days of thought
  • Padding the calendar. Meetings with the wrong people look good in a report and waste your seller's time
  • No access. If you cannot see the replies, you cannot learn from them

Trust your own read of the conversation. If the provider asks sharp questions, explains its process and is clear about what it does not know, that is a good sign.

What to get in writing before you sign with an outsourced SDR provider

A clear agreement protects both sides. It also forces the provider to say what it will actually do. Use this list as a starting point for the conversation. It is a planning aid, not legal advice, so have a qualified lawyer read the final terms.

  • Scope. The channels, the audiences, the tasks included and the tasks that cost extra
  • Meeting definition. The written description of a qualified meeting and how disputes are settled
  • Start and setup. What happens before sending begins and what you must provide
  • Approvals. Who signs off on messages and how fast each side responds
  • Changes. What happens if the first approach needs to change, and how scope changes are agreed
  • Reporting and access. What you will see, how often and in which tools
  • Ownership. Who keeps the list, the domains, the mailboxes, the conversation history and the opt-out records
  • Fees. What the fee covers, what is extra and when payment is due
  • Pause rights. Whether you can pause the work and what happens to the fee if you do
  • Term and notice. How long the agreement runs, how renewal works and how to end it
  • Wind-down. What the provider hands over when the work stops and how open conversations are handled
  • Data and privacy. How contact data is sourced, stored and protected
  • Brand use. What the provider may say in your name and what needs your approval first

Read each point and ask whether the answer is written or only spoken. If it is only spoken, ask for it in the document.

Look at what is missing too. If the paper says nothing about ownership, assume nothing. If it says nothing about what happens when the first approach needs to change, ask. A first campaign often needs adjustment, and a rigid agreement can turn that into a dispute.

Be careful with promises on paper as well. A line that states a meeting count or a result date sounds reassuring. Ask what happens if it is not met and who decides whether the cause was the market, the offer or the provider. Clear process matters more than a number on a page.

Finally, keep a copy of everything you sent the provider: the buyer definition, the offer, the exclusions and the approved messages. If something goes wrong, those papers show what was agreed.

How to end or switch an outsourced SDR provider without losing momentum

Most startups will change how they run outbound at some point. You might switch providers, bring the work in house or pause for a while. Done well, a change keeps what you learned. Done badly, you start over.

Common reasons to make a change include a shift in strategy, a wish for more control, a poor fit between the provider and your market or a team ready to run outbound itself. None of these needs blame. What matters is how orderly the exit is.

Review the agreement first. Check the notice terms, the wind-down terms and the ownership terms. Know what you are entitled to before you start the conversation. Give notice in the way the agreement describes.

Ask for a handover. A good handover includes:

  • The contact list with the status of each person
  • Every reply thread and booked meeting record
  • Notes on objections, phrases and lessons from the work
  • The approved messages and any test results
  • Access to or transfer of sending domains and mailboxes, as agreed
  • The opt-out record
  • Any calendar links and routing rules in use

Protect the people already in conversation. Some buyers are mid-thread when the change happens. Decide who answers them and from which address. A sudden silence or a message from an unknown sender can lose a warm lead.

Protect your sending reputation. Moving sending setups can affect whether your messages reach inboxes. Plan the move with care, and never use the exit as a reason to send a large batch fast.

Respect opt-outs. Anyone who asked to stop must stay stopped, even with a new sender. Carry the opt-out record to the next team.

Brief the next team. Whether it is a new provider or your own hire, give them what you learned: the buyer, the objections, the best messages and the meetings that went well. That saves them from repeating the early experiments.

Consider an overlap if the agreement allows it. A short period where both sides work together can make the handoff smoother. If a clean break is better, plan the dates and responsibilities in writing.

Last, look back before you move on. Ask what worked, what did not and what you would do differently next time. That review makes the next program better, whoever runs it.

Frequently asked questions

What is an outsourced SDR for a startup?

An outsourced SDR for a startup is an outside provider that handles outbound prospecting for you. The provider builds contact lists, writes and sends messages by email, LinkedIn and phone, handles every reply and sets qualified meetings on your calendar. Your founders or sellers then run the conversation and the close, so you get sales meetings without hiring and managing a rep yourself.

When should a startup choose outsourced SDR over an in-house hire?

Choose outsourced SDR when you know your buyer but lack the time or setup to prospect every day. It suits founders who are too busy to run outbound, teams that want to test a motion before hiring and companies that do not want to manage a rep. Hire in-house when your product is complex and you want one person to learn it deeply.

Is a startup ready for outsourced SDR before product-market fit?

Sometimes. Before product-market fit, outsourced outbound works as a structured test of who responds to your offer. It needs a clear buyer, a stable pitch and someone to read the replies. It does not work if the offer changes constantly or the product is not ready for customers. Treat it as research, not as proof that the product fits.

What do I need to give an outsourced SDR provider?

You give the provider your ideal customer profile, a plain description of your offer, any proof you can share and the exclusions for accounts you do not want contacted. You also give calendar access, rules for what counts as a qualified meeting and one contact who can approve messages quickly. The clearer these inputs, the better the targeting and the messages.

What does the first stretch of outsourced SDR look like?

The first stretch focuses on setup, list building, messaging and first conversations. The provider agrees your buyer definition, prepares sending infrastructure and calendar access, builds a contact list and drafts messages for your approval. Sending starts carefully. Replies show what to adjust. Timing varies with your market and how fast inputs arrive, so no provider should promise exact results.

What drives the cost of outsourced SDR compared with hiring?

Hiring cost comes from pay, benefits, recruiting, tools, manager time and the risk of replacing a rep. Outsourced cost depends on the channels used, the number of buyers targeted, the list work involved, how much reply handling and booking is included and the agreement length. Ask each provider for a written scope so you can compare them fairly.

How do I choose an outsourced SDR provider?

Ask what is included, how they define a qualified meeting, how they protect your sending domain and how they build lists. Ask what you will see, what the agreement requires and who you will talk to. Be cautious of promised results, vague scope and generic messages. Reference calls with companies that have used the provider are the most useful check.

Can an outsourced provider replace my founder-led sales?

No. A provider replaces the repetitive prospecting work, not the founder's judgment. Founders should keep taking early meetings, hearing buyer feedback and approving the offer. The provider fills the calendar with relevant conversations. You still own the product knowledge, the relationship and the close, which is why a clear handoff matters.

Who owns the contact list and sending domains in an outsourced SDR program?

It depends on the agreement, so settle it in writing before work starts. Ask who owns the list. Ask who registers the sending domains and mailboxes. Ask who keeps the reply history and opt-out records. Also ask what the provider hands over when the work ends. If a provider will not answer clearly, treat that as a warning sign.

How do outsourced SDR providers charge?

Most use one of three models. A retainer is a regular fee for a defined scope. Per-meeting pricing charges when a meeting is booked and accepted. A hybrid combines a base fee with a variable part. Each model moves risk differently. Ask every provider to price the same scope. Check what counts as billable and what is extra.

What should I get in writing before hiring an outsourced SDR provider?

Get the scope, the meeting definition, who approves messages, what reports you will see and who owns the data and sending setup. Add the fees and extras, how to pause, the notice terms and what the provider hands over at the end. Treat this as a planning list and have a lawyer read the final agreement.

How does outsourced SDR work differ from appointment setting?

An outsourced SDR covers the whole front end of outbound: targeting, list building, messaging, sending, reply handling and booking. Appointment setting is usually narrower and focuses on getting the meeting agreed, sometimes from a list you supply. Ask any provider exactly where the scope starts and stops before you compare them.

Should a startup use an AI SDR tool or a person-led provider?

It depends on how much judgment your outbound needs. Software handles repeatable tasks well. People are better at reading replies, spotting a bad fit and deciding when not to send. Whatever you choose, make sure someone reviews messages, protects your sending reputation and answers for mistakes. You also need someone on your side to handle replies.

What is a qualified meeting for a startup outbound program?

It is a call with the right person at the right kind of company who faces a real problem you solve and agreed to talk. Write that definition down before outreach begins. Also agree on how no-shows, reschedules and wrong-fit contacts are handled, so you and the provider measure the same thing.

How do I switch from one outsourced SDR provider to another?

Check your notice and wind-down terms first. Ask for the contact list, the reply threads, the opt-out record, your message copy and any sending setup you own. Decide who answers buyers who are mid-thread. Then brief the new provider on what you learned so it does not repeat early tests. Keep opt-outs respected throughout.
