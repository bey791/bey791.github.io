Cold SMS vs cold email for B2B outreach

By Ahmad Khan, Founder. Published and updated November 17, 2026.

Cold SMS vs cold email is mostly a consent question. In the US, texting a prospect who never agreed to hear from you needs prior consent under the TCPA, so cold email opens the conversation and SMS follows a reply.

The short answer: email opens, SMS follows

If you sell to businesses in the US and you are weighing a first touch by text against a first touch by email, the choice is mostly made for you. A marketing text to a mobile number that never opted in carries legal risk that a well built cold email does not. Email can introduce you to a stranger. A text should land only after that stranger has written back and agreed to continue by phone.

That does not make SMS a weak channel. It is a fast and personal one, which is exactly why the rules around it are strict. Used at the right moment, a text turns a slow email thread into a booked call. Used too early, it turns a prospect into a complaint. This guide covers what each channel is good at, what the law expects of each and the sequence we run so that both pull in the same direction.

This is general information about US rules, not legal advice. Have counsel review your consent language and your sending plan before any program goes live.

What the TCPA means for texting a cold prospect

The Telephone Consumer Protection Act and the FCC rules written under it treat a text message much like a phone call. According to the FCC's consumer guide to robocalls and texts, the rules ban texts to a mobile phone sent with an autodialer unless the owner gave consent first. Commercial texts require written consent. The same guide notes that these protections apply even when the number is not on the National Do Not Call Registry.

In plain terms, a sales text to someone who never gave you their number for that purpose is the kind of message these rules were written to stop. Buying a list of mobile numbers does not create consent. Finding a number on a company website or in a data tool does not create consent either. Consent has to come from the person who owns the phone and it has to cover the kind of message you plan to send.

The cited FCC guide and 47 CFR 64.1200 talk about texts sent to a mobile phone and about consent for commercial messages. Neither one writes a special workplace exception. Most decision makers list a personal mobile as their direct line, so we treat a B2B text to that phone as needing prior consent. Counsel should confirm how the rules apply to your list.

The rules also give people a way out. The FCC guide says a recipient may opt out of robotexts at any time and in any reasonable manner, even after giving consent. A reply such as "please stop" counts, not just the keyword STOP. Our SMS compliance checklist walks through consent records, registration and opt outs in more detail.

What the law asks of a cold email

Commercial email in the US is governed mainly by the CAN-SPAM Act, which the FTC enforces. The rule that implements it sits at 16 CFR Part 316. Unlike the texting rules, CAN-SPAM does not require consent before the first message. It sets conditions on how the message is sent instead.

The FTC's CAN-SPAM compliance guide lists the core duties. Header and routing details must be accurate. The subject line cannot mislead. The message must say it is an advertisement where that applies, include a valid postal address and explain how to opt out. The FTC gives senders 10 business days to act on an opt out request. The same guide states that commercial email to a business recipient still has to follow these duties, including a one to one sales note to a buyer.

That difference in structure is the whole story of this comparison. Email law asks you to identify yourself and stop when asked. Texting rules ask you to get permission before you start. Our guide on whether cold email is legal covers the email side, including how other countries differ.

Cold SMS and cold email side by side

Put the two channels next to each other and the gap is clear. Each line below is about a first message to someone who has not heard from you before.

  • Consent before the first message: email needs none under CAN-SPAM; a marketing text to a US mobile number needs prior consent under the TCPA
  • What the reader expects: inboxes are where people expect to hear from vendors; a phone is where people expect to hear from friends, family and colleagues
  • Room to explain: an email can carry a short reason, a relevant example and a clear ask; a text has room for a sentence or two
  • Identity: an email shows a name, a company domain and a signature; a text from an unknown number shows almost nothing until the reader opens it
  • Opting out: email uses an unsubscribe link or a reply; a text uses STOP or any reasonable request, which must be honored
  • Cost of a mistake: a poorly targeted email is ignored or filtered; an unwanted text can turn into a complaint to the carrier or a legal claim
  • Speed once a buyer is engaged: email threads can stall for days; texts tend to be read soon after they arrive

Read down the list and a pattern shows up. Email is built for the first approach. SMS is built for the moments after the first approach has worked.

Where cold email wins

Cold email is the right opening channel for most B2B sales because it lets you earn attention without asking for anything personal. A buyer can read your note at their desk, forward it to a colleague or file it for next quarter. None of that feels like an intrusion.

  • Reach at the start: you can contact a well researched list of companies that match your buyer profile without needing their permission first
  • Context: a few short lines can show you understand their market, which a text cannot do without feeling pushy
  • Forwarding: emails travel inside a buying committee; texts rarely do
  • Testing: subject lines, openers and offers can be compared across segments over a few weeks
  • Record keeping: every reply sits in a thread you can review, hand over or reopen months later

Email also pairs well with other first touch channels. If you are weighing it against LinkedIn rather than text, see our comparison of cold email and LinkedIn outreach.

Where SMS wins once a buyer says yes

A text is short, personal and hard to miss. Those traits make it a poor cold opener and a very good closer. Once a buyer has replied and agreed to talk by phone, SMS removes friction that email adds.

  • Scheduling: picking a time by text is quicker than a chain of emails across two calendars
  • Reminders: a short note the day before a call can help cut no shows
  • Same day questions: a buyer who wants a quick answer before a meeting can get one without opening a laptop
  • Reviving a quiet thread: if a prospect who agreed to texts goes silent, a short check in often gets a faster answer than another email
  • Event follow up: people who gave you their number at a trade show booth for that purpose can be reached the same evening

Every case on that list starts with a buyer who has already engaged. That is the line we hold. The speed of SMS is a reward for consent, not a shortcut around it.

The sequence we use: email first, text after a reply

Here is how the two channels fit together in the outbound programs we run. The order matters more than the copy.

  • Step one, research: build a list of companies and contacts that fit the buyer profile, with verified business email addresses
  • Step two, cold email: send a short, relevant first note from a warmed sending domain with a clear way to opt out
  • Step three, follow up by email: a few spaced follow ups in the same thread, each adding something new
  • Step four, the reply: when a buyer answers with interest, ask how they prefer to keep in touch and whether a text is welcome
  • Step five, record consent: save the reply that grants permission, with the date, the number given and what they agreed to receive
  • Step six, SMS: use text for scheduling, reminders and quick questions, from a registered number, with your business name in the first message
  • Step seven, honor stops: any request to stop texting ends texting at once and the thread moves back to email

This is the same order we follow inside our full cycle outbound programs, where email and LinkedIn lead and SMS is added only for contacts who opted in.

How to ask for permission to text in a reply

Most teams skip this step because it feels awkward. It is the step that makes the rest of the program safe. Asking is easy when it is framed around the buyer's convenience rather than yours.

A good permission ask has three parts. It names what you will send, it gives the buyer a real choice and it lets them pick the number. For example: "Happy to find a time. Would a text be easier than email for scheduling? If so, send the best mobile number and I will only use it for this meeting." The buyer who replies with a number has now chosen the channel for a specific purpose.

Keep the scope honest. Permission to text about a meeting is not permission to add the number to a monthly promotional blast. If you want to send broader marketing texts later, ask for that separately and in writing. Keep the record.

Store the evidence where your team can find it. The email thread that contains the buyer's yes, a note of the number they gave and the date are what you would need if anyone ever asked how that number came to receive texts.

What the first text after a reply should say

The first message sets the tone for the whole thread. It should feel like a continuation of the email conversation, not a new campaign.

  • Your name and company: the buyer may not have saved your number, so say who you are
  • The reason for the text: point back to the email thread and the agreed purpose
  • One clear ask: a time to confirm, a question to answer or a link to a calendar
  • A way out: a short note that replying STOP ends texts

A sample, with invented names: "Hi Dana, it's Sam from Northfield Freight, following up on our email about route planning. Does Thursday at 2 or Friday at 10 suit you? Reply STOP to opt out." It is short, it names the sender and it points back to the agreed purpose.

Avoid links from public URL shorteners, all capital letters and anything that reads like an ad. Carriers screen business messages and those patterns are the ones most likely to be filtered.

Cold texting vs cold calling

People who search this topic often ask a related question: if texting a stranger is risky, is a cold call any better? The two are not the same. A live, manually dialed call to a business line is treated differently from an automated marketing text to a mobile phone. Many B2B teams still run calling alongside email.

Calling has its own rules. The FCC guide says telemarketing calls to a home are barred before 8 a.m. and after 9 p.m. local time, the National Do Not Call Registry covers landline and wireless numbers and prerecorded telemarketing calls need prior written consent. If you call mobile numbers, check them against the registry and keep an internal do not call list.

In practice, calling and texting play different roles. A call can open a conversation with a business line and a text can confirm the meeting that call produced, once the buyer agrees. Neither replaces email as the most scalable first touch for most B2B offers.

Mistakes that turn a good lead into a complaint

  • Texting scraped or purchased numbers: no consent exists, however well matched the list is
  • Treating a business card as permission: a number printed on a card or a signature is contact information, not consent to marketing texts
  • Moving to text because email went quiet: silence is not a yes; follow up by email or try another channel
  • Stretching a narrow yes: a buyer who agreed to texts about one meeting has not signed up for promotions
  • Sending from an unregistered number: carriers filter unregistered business traffic, so messages vanish and your number suffers
  • Ignoring a soft stop: "not interested, thanks" by text should be treated as a request to stop
  • Texting outside working hours: a late evening text to a buyer's personal phone undoes the goodwill the email built

Each of these mistakes saves a little time and adds a lot of risk. A program that holds the line on consent tends to get warmer replies too, because buyers can tell the difference between a vendor who asked and one who assumed.

Tools and setup for the SMS side

The SMS tool we use is ClickSend. Whichever platform you choose, the setup questions are the same. Answer them before the first text goes out.

  • Registration: US carriers expect business texts from standard ten digit numbers to be registered for A2P 10DLC with The Campaign Registry, a step most texting platforms handle for you
  • Carrier guidance: the CTIA messaging principles describe what carriers expect around consent, opt outs and content
  • Opt out handling: STOP and HELP replies should be processed by the platform and synced to your records
  • Consent storage: each number should carry a note of when and how permission was given
  • Inbox links: replies by text should land somewhere the person running the email thread can see them

On the email side, the tools matter less than the setup: separate sending domains, proper authentication, warmed inboxes and a verified list. Get that right and the email channel will produce the replies the SMS channel depends on.

How to measure the two channels together

Because email and SMS play different roles, judge them on different numbers and then look at the handoff between them.

  • Email, first touch: delivery, positive reply rate and meetings booked per segment
  • The handoff: the share of positive replies that accept the offer to continue by text
  • SMS, after consent: time from first text to a confirmed meeting, show rate for meetings confirmed by text and opt out rate
  • Quality checks: carrier filtering, complaints and any number that should not have been texted

If the opt out rate on texts climbs, the problem is usually upstream: the permission ask was too vague or texts went beyond what the buyer agreed to. Fix the ask before you change the message.

Frequently asked questions

Is cold texting legal for B2B in the US?

Marketing texts to a US mobile number need the owner's prior consent under the TCPA and FCC rules. Commercial texts need written consent. The FCC guide and 47 CFR 64.1200 talk about the mobile phone and the message type, not a special B2B exception. A cold text to a prospect who never opted in still carries legal risk. This is general information, not legal advice.

Can I text a number I found on a company website or in a data tool?

Finding a number does not create consent. Use that contact to send an email first, then ask in the reply whether a text is welcome and keep a record of the answer.

Is cold email legal in the US?

It is, when it follows CAN-SPAM. Use accurate headers and an honest subject line, include a postal address, explain how to opt out and act on opt out requests within 10 business days. The FTC guide states that commercial email to a business recipient still has to follow these duties.

Should SMS or email come first in a B2B sequence?

Email comes first. It can reach a new prospect lawfully and with enough context to earn a reply. SMS follows once the buyer has replied and agreed to continue by text.

What SMS tool does Be Leaded use?

We use ClickSend for SMS, on registered numbers and only for contacts who agreed to receive texts.

Can SMS replace cold email for outbound?

Not in the US. Without prior consent a text is the wrong first touch. SMS works best as the channel that speeds up scheduling and reminders after email has started the conversation.
