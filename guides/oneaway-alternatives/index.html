OneAway on its own pages

The notes that follow start with OneAway as the baseline. This is not a score, and no firm paid to appear. The OneAway homepage, the services page and the OneAway pricing page were opened on November 12, 2026.

Those pages describe a done-for-you mail and LinkedIn program aimed at B2B SaaS teams, with meetings booked onto the calendar. Intro is cold email only. Managed and Scale add LinkedIn. All three plans have a three-month minimum, paid monthly, then continue month to month. The pricing page states there is no setup fee.

Sending domains and inboxes are set up and warmed, and the company's main domain is not used for cold email. Interested replies are handled and meetings are placed on the calendar. The same pages say OneAway does not cold call. Warm calling is listed as an optional add-on after a person has already replied with interest. The About page states that the firm was founded in June 2022.

When someone writes back

Vendors tend to describe one of three follow-up routes after a positive reply. In the first, the vendor qualifies the response and places the meeting. In the second, the vendor passes the interested reply back to you. In the third, the route depends on which plan you bought.

This section names a vendor only when that vendor's site describes the reply path. OneAway says interested replies are handled and meetings are booked onto the calendar. The email setup is on our cold email agency writeup. The LinkedIn setup is on the LinkedIn lead generation writeup. Booking a time is described on the appointment setting page.

Rent or own: what you keep when the contract ends

Two programs can look alike while you are still paying, then split apart the week the work stops. The useful test is simple. What do you still hold, in accounts you can log into, once the vendor is no longer on the job?

Use this as a short checklist, then take each line into the contract. The firm notes above already record the few leave policies that were printed on the sites we opened. This list is wider, because many of those pages are silent on one or more of the items.

  • Sending domains, including the DNS records that make those domains able to send mail (SPF, DKIM and DMARC). If the vendor registered the domain, ask whether it will be transferred into a registrar account you control, and how the cutover is done.
  • Mailboxes used for outreach, plus the warmup history and any placement notes tied to those inboxes. A screenshot of a dashboard is not the same as admin access.
  • The prospect list, in a file you can open without the vendor's tool. Ask for the fields, the source of each row and the date the row was last checked.
  • The copy: first emails, follow-ups, LinkedIn messages, call openers and any snippets the team reused. Ask whether you may keep using that copy after the term ends.
  • The working data: reply threads, meeting notes, tags, suppression lists and the reasons a person was removed from outreach.

A common pattern is a packed export and a thin handoff. You receive a spreadsheet of names and a folder of approved drafts, yet the sending domain stays in the vendor's registrar, the mailboxes close and the suppression file never arrives. The next partner then has to rebuild the infrastructure and may write again to people who already asked to be left alone.

Ownership is not a vibe. It is a set of logins, registrar records, file formats and written rights. If a vendor says you will "get everything," ask them to name the five items above, one by one, and to say who is the account owner on each tool. If they cannot name the owner, you do not own that layer yet.

It also helps to split "yours to download" from "yours to operate." A CSV of leads is useful. It does not let you keep sending from the same warmed mailboxes, and it does not let you edit DNS if the domain was never in your registrar. The opposite problem shows up too. You may already hold the domains, then discover that the sequences, the Clay tables or the inbox rotation live only in a vendor workspace you will lose on the last day.

Before you sign, ask for a leave plan in writing. Who turns over which assets and in what format? On what notice? Who pays for domain transfer fees, leftover mailbox seats or a final export? What must be deleted on their side, and how do they confirm that deletion? You do not need a dramatic exit story. You need a list you can hand to whoever runs the next program, including your own staff if you bring the work in-house.

Be Leaded's public program pages are plain on a smaller set of these points. The month to month lead generation page states that cold volume never goes out from your company domain. The lists and replies are yours. The term is spelled out in the live month-to-month terms. The next section records the leave lines we actually found on OneAway's site and on Be Leaded's site, and it repeats the four questions worth putting to every other firm on this list.

What to keep if you leave

Before you serve notice, get these four points in writing: who retains the prospect file, who retains the mail threads, who retains the CRM rows and who retains control of the sending domains. Put those questions to OneAway and to the rest of the list.

OneAway's pricing page states that a full export is available at any time, and that interested leads stay yours if you leave. On Be Leaded programs, the lists and replies are yours. A file you can download is not the same as holding the DNS records for the sending domain. If only the vendor can edit those records, the domain stays behind.

How to choose an outbound model

The firm notes and the grid above compare published channels, delivery models and terms. They will not, on their own, tell you which model fits the way you want to work. That choice sits in three practical questions. Who should do the work, and for how long do you want to depend on them? Do you want to reach a wide market quickly, or a smaller set of accounts with tighter research? And will the work run in tools you already pay for, or in a stack the vendor prefers?

Answer those in writing before you compare logos. Two proposals can both say "done for you" and still be different products: one is a rented motion that stops when the invoices stop, and the other is a build you can keep running. The sections after this one go deeper on hybrids, high-volume email, meeting-count commercial models and work done inside your own accounts. Start here so those later notes have a place to sit.

Agency, in-house team or an implementation partner

An agency, in the sense used on this page, is hired to run outreach for you. They usually build the list, write the copy, handle replies and, where their site says so, place meetings on a calendar. You pay a fee, they do the daily work and you stay close to the conversations that matter. The strength is speed and a team that already knows the motion. The trade-off is dependence. If the contract ends and you do not hold the domains, the mailboxes, the list and the copy, the motion is hard to restart.

An in-house team keeps the work on your payroll. You hire or assign people, you buy the tools, you approve the copy and you live with the ramp. The strength is control and the memory that stays in your company when a campaign is over. The trade-off is time, hiring, management and the quiet cost of keeping inboxes healthy. In-house is a good fit when you already have someone who can own list quality, deliverability and reply handling, and when you want that skill to remain after any vendor leaves.

An implementation partner is a third path that buyers sometimes skip. You hire a firm to design the workflow, stand it up in your accounts, write down how it runs and show your staff how to operate it. The daily sending may still be done by the partner for a while, or it may pass to you once the build is stable. The product you are buying is a system you can keep, not only a month of activity. The trade-off is that you need people who will actually run what was built. A documented Clay table that no one on your side will open is not ownership in practice.

None of these three is automatically better. A young sales team that has never run cold email may want an agency first, then revisit ownership after they have seen what a healthy program looks like. A team that already has operators, or that has been burned by a program they could not restart, may want a partner who builds in their accounts from day one. A team with spare capacity and a clear offer may want to stay in-house and hire only for a narrow gap, such as inbox setup or list research.

When you read a proposal, ask which of the three you are actually being sold. Some firms describe themselves as a partner and then run the entire motion in their own workspaces. Some describe themselves as an agency and will still place domains and lists in your name if you ask. The label on the homepage is less useful than a written answer to three questions. Who does the daily work this month? Who will be able to do it if we stop paying? And what, exactly, changes hands on the last day?

Be Leaded's published offer, described on the lead generation services page and in the live month-to-month terms, is a month-to-month outbound program. Email and LinkedIn sit inside that program. Calling is quoted separately. That is an agency motion with no 3-month commitment, pay month by month, that you can cancel at the end of any month, and with the lists and replies kept by you. It is not described on those pages as a Clay build or as a training engagement that turns your staff into the operators. If that is the model you want, say so on the call and compare it with firms that publish a handoff of that kind.

Volume or precision

The second choice is how wide you want to go. Some teams want to reach a large market on a steady cadence, with enough inboxes and enough list coverage to keep the calendar filled from a broad pool. Other teams want a smaller set of accounts, richer research on each row and a message that refers to something specific about that company. Both can be honest ways to work. They are not the same program, and they should not be priced or staffed as if they were.

A volume program lives or dies on infrastructure and on list hygiene. You need enough warmed sending domains and mailboxes, a way to rotate them, a way to verify addresses before a send and a way to notice when a domain is in trouble. You also need enough people, or a tight workflow, to read the replies that a large send will produce. A pretty sequence does not help if the mail does not arrive, or if interested replies sit unopened.

A precision program lives or dies on the quality of the research and on the definition of a good account. You need a tight buyer profile, a reason to write this month rather than next month and a message that can survive a careful reader. You also need the patience to accept that fewer sends will go out, and a way to judge the work that is not "how many emails left the building."

Mixing the two without saying so is where programs get mushy. A vendor may talk about deep research, then send a generic first line at a scale that no researcher could have touched. Another may talk about scale, then under-invest in inboxes so that the volume they promised never actually lands. Ask to see a sample of the list and a sample of the first message, and ask how many people will look at a row before it is sent. If the answer is "the system personalizes every line," ask what the system is allowed to use, and who reviews a sample before launch.

Your market should drive the choice. If the set of companies that can buy from you is small, burning that set with thin copy is expensive in a way that does not show up on a sending report. If the set is large and the offer is easy to understand, a careful volume program may be the honest fit. Write down which one you want before you look at a channel mix, because email, LinkedIn and calling can all be used either way.

Their stack or yours

The third choice is where the work lives. Some vendors run outreach in their own tools, under their own accounts, and give you a report or a portal. That can be tidy. It can also mean that the Clay tables, the sequencer, the inbox rotation and the enrichment waterfalls are not yours to keep. Other vendors will work inside the tools you already pay for, or will open those tools in your name from the start, so that admin access never leaves your company.

Ask who is the account owner on every system that will touch a prospect. That includes the registrar, the mailbox host, the sending platform, the enrichment tool, the LinkedIn sending tool if one is used, Clay or any other workflow tool and the CRM. "We will share a login" is not the same as "the account is in your workspace, billed to your card, with your staff as owner." Shared logins also make it harder to cut off access cleanly when the term ends.

There is a real cost to insisting on your stack. Onboarding can take longer. The vendor may have to adapt a playbook they usually run in a tool they know well. You may have to buy seats you were hoping to avoid. The gain is continuity. When the engagement ends, your staff still has the sequences, the data and the sending reputation that was built in your name.

There is also a cost to accepting their stack without looking. You may get a smoother first month. You may then find that switching partners, or bringing the work in-house, means starting the infrastructure over. If you accept that trade, do it with open eyes, and still ask for an export of the list, the copy and the reply history, plus a written rule on who may keep writing to those people.

A practical way to hold this conversation is to draw a simple map. On one side, the tools you already own. On the other, the tools the vendor wants to add. For each box, write "ours," "theirs" or "shared," and write who can export or transfer it. Bring that map to the call. It will surface disagreements early, which is cheaper than surfacing them in a notice letter.

Hybrid programs and training

Some firms sell a blend rather than a pure agency month or a pure handoff. They run the program for you, and they also teach your staff how the system works, with the stated aim that you can take more of the work later. Other firms sell a short build and a training period, then step back to a lighter advisory role. These offers can be a good middle path. They can also be a regular managed program with a few walkthrough calls added at the end, which is a weaker form of the same idea.

If training is part of what you think you are buying, make it specific. Who is being trained and in which tools? On which tasks? List building, copy, inbox health, reply handling and reporting are different skills, and a single recorded demo does not cover them all. Ask whether the training is written down, so a new hire on your side can follow it, or whether it lives only in a call that no one recorded.

Ask when your staff will first do the work themselves, while the vendor is still available to review it. A handoff that happens only after the last invoice is a hard way to learn. A better pattern is that your people start running one part of the motion, such as list review or reply handling, while the vendor still runs the rest, and that this shift is written into the plan rather than promised in passing.

Documentation is the test that survives staff changes. You want a written description of the workflow, the naming of the tables and segments, the rules for when a person leaves a sequence, the DNS and mailbox setup and the way a weekly review is done. You also want to know who can edit that documentation. If only the vendor can update it, the document will drift the moment they leave.

Hybrid offers should still pass the rent-or-own checklist. Training in a workspace you cannot keep is a tour, not a transfer. If the partner is serious about leaving you able to operate the motion, the accounts will be yours, the docs will be yours and the training will be timed while they are still on the hook to fix gaps. Put that in the statement of work, including how many sessions, on which topics and what "done" looks like for each one.

Be honest about your own capacity too. A training plan that assumes your sales lead will spend a large share of the week inside a sequencer will fail if that person is already in meetings all day. If you do not have someone who can own the system after the partner steps back, you are still buying a managed program, and you should judge it that way. There is no shame in that. There is risk in pretending you will operate a build you cannot staff.

High-volume email programs

Some outbound offers are built to send a great deal of email, week after week, to a large addressable market. That is a legitimate product when the market is truly wide, the offer is easy to grasp in a few lines and the vendor has the infrastructure to send without wrecking placement. It is a poor fit when your buyer pool is small, when each message needs a long setup or when your team cannot handle a large number of replies with care.

Infrastructure is the first thing to inspect. High-volume sending usually needs many domains and many mailboxes. Those inboxes should be warmed before real outreach, rotated so that no single inbox carries the whole load and watched for signs of trouble. Ask who registers the domains, who owns the registrar account, how warmup is done and what happens when a domain starts to struggle. Ask whether your primary corporate domain will be kept out of the sending pool. On Be Leaded programs, cold volume stays off the domain your staff already use for day to day mail. Other firms should be able to say the same, in writing.

List quality is the second thing. Volume does not excuse a dirty file. Ask how addresses are verified, how catch-all or risky rows are treated, how often a person can be written to and how bounce or complaint events get back into the list. Ask who maintains the suppression file, including people who replied, people who asked to be left alone and people who already booked or already said no. A high-volume program that does not protect that file will spend its later months writing to the same tired rows, or worse, to people who should never have been written to again.

Reply handling is the third thing, and it is the one that gets skipped in a pitch about sending capacity. A large send produces more replies, including polite interest, confusion, anger and out-of-office noise. Someone has to read those, sort them and act. Ask who is on the inbox and during which hours. Ask what the written rule is for a positive reply. If meetings are part of the offer, ask who books them and how quickly a reply is picked up. The appointment setting page describes how Be Leaded frames a booked time. Other firms should be willing to put their own rule in writing.

Copy at volume is a fourth pressure point. Some programs use a short, stable sequence and spend their energy on the list and on deliverability. Some try to vary each first line with research or with a generated snippet. Both can be done well or badly. Ask to see a week of actual first lines, not a single polished sample, and ask who is allowed to change the sequence. If a generated line is allowed to go out without a review, ask what the system is forbidden to say, and how you will hear about a bad send.

Judge a high-volume offer by whether the vendor will talk plainly about placement, list hygiene and reply capacity, not by how large the sending number sounds on a call. If those three topics are waved away, the volume is a vanity line. If they are answered in writing, you can then decide whether a wide program is the right fit for your market, or whether a smaller, more researched motion would waste fewer of the accounts you actually care about.

One more practical check: what happens if you need to slow down or pause. A machine built for a wide send may be awkward to turn down without leaving domains idle or lists half-used. Ask how a pause is handled, who keeps warming the inboxes during a quiet period and whether you can shrink the file without a fight. You want a program you can steer, not only a program you can start.

Offers built around a meeting count

Some firms sell outreach. Some sell a commercial model in which a stated number of meetings is the thing you are paying for, or in which a fee is tied to meetings that are held. Those offers can be clear and fair when the definition is tight and the paperwork is honest. They can also hide the real product behind a number, so this section stays with the definition and the process, and it does not repeat any count, pace or fee printed on a vendor site.

Start with what "a meeting" means. Is it a time that was booked, a time that was held, or a time that met a written buyer profile? Does a no-show count? Does a cancellation by the buyer count? Does a call with the wrong title count? Does a meeting that your team later rejects count? If two people from the same company show up, is that one meeting or two? Write the answers down. If the vendor cannot answer them before you sign, you do not yet know what you are buying.

Then ask who decides that a meeting qualifies. If the vendor both books the time and judges whether it met the standard, you need a written way to dispute a row, and a time window in which to do it. If you judge the meeting, you need the same window, and you need the notes from the call in a place you can find. A weekly list of "meetings delivered" with no definition attached is not a useful report.

Ask what happens when a meeting is missed, canceled or a poor match. Some firms will book a replacement. Some will not bill that row. Some will count it anyway. The right answer is the one written in the agreement, not the one said on a pitch call. Also ask whether unused meetings in a period vanish, carry forward or change the next invoice. Again, this page does not print those commercial terms for any firm. It only tells you to collect them.

A meeting-count offer should still be clear on the rent-or-own questions. You can receive a full calendar and still own none of the list, the domains or the copy. If the program stops, the next month's meetings stop with it, and you may have little to restart. That may be acceptable if you wanted a short test and you understood the trade. It is a hard surprise if you thought you were also buying an engine.

Watch for a mismatch between the way meetings are sold and the way the market actually buys. If your sale involves several people and a long review, a first conversation may be valuable even when it is not a clean "meeting" under a narrow definition. If you sign up to a count that ignores those conversations, the vendor may steer toward short, easy bookings that look good in the report and help you less. Ask to see how the definition would treat a messy but real conversation, and whether you can change the definition in writing after the first cycle if it is a poor fit.

Finally, keep the meeting model separate from the rest of the pitch. Channels, terms, reply handling and ownership still matter. A clean definition of a meeting does not repair a list you cannot take with you, or a sending domain you cannot control. Use the grid and the firm notes for those facts, and use this section only to interrogate the commercial model around the calendar.

Clay and RevOps work in your own accounts

A different kind of offer has become common beside the classic done-for-you desk. A firm is hired to design workflows in Clay, to clean and enrich records, to connect those workflows to your CRM and sometimes to send or route the results, with the work done inside accounts that you own. The pitch is that you keep the tables, the enrichments and the routing when the engagement ends. That can be true. It is true only if the accounts, the billing and the admin rights are actually yours.

Start by naming the workspace. Who created the Clay workspace, whose email is the owner and whose card is on the bill? The same questions apply to the CRM, the sequencer, the data providers and any automation tool that sits between them. If the partner built a beautiful set of tables in a workspace they own, what you have at the end is a demonstration, plus perhaps an export. If they built the same tables in a workspace your company owns, with your staff as administrators, you have something you can keep operating.

RevOps work in this setting is the layer that keeps the records honest and the handoff usable. That can include ongoing enrichment, the merging of duplicate people and companies, the scoring or routing of a new reply or form fill, the push of context into the CRM so a seller can take a call and the reporting that shows where conversations came from. None of that has to be mysterious. Each piece should have a name, a trigger, an owner and a place in your own tools.

Ask how much of the workflow is standard and how much is built around your process. A standard playbook can be fast to launch. A custom build can respect a CRM you are not willing to abandon, a data source you already trust or a routing rule your sales lead will not give up. The cost of a custom build is more decisions, and the need for someone on your side to take part in them. If you do not have that person, you may be happier with a managed program that does not pretend to leave a system behind.

Documentation matters here even more than in a simple email program, because the work is easy to lose in a web of tables. You want a written map of the main tables, the key enrichments, the rules that move a person from one step to the next and the way a seller is notified. You want that map in a document you store, not only in a comment field the partner can see. You also want a short "what to do when this breaks" note for the usual failures: an enrichment that stops, a mailbox that is paused, a routing rule that fails to fire.

Training belongs with this model if the point is that your staff will operate the system. Use the same tests set out in the hybrid section. Who is trained and on which tasks? With which written guides? When do they first do the work while the partner is still available? If no one on your side will live in Clay or in the CRM admin after the partner leaves, the build will sit still. In that case an honest managed program may waste less money than an unused system.

Be Leaded does not publish a Clay or RevOps implementation offer on the pages linked from this guide. The live pages describe a managed outbound program: list work, copy, email, LinkedIn, calling quoted separately, reply handling and booking, run month to month, with the lists and replies kept by you. If you want a system built in your Clay workspace and your CRM, say that plainly when you compare firms, and judge Be Leaded on the offer it actually publishes, not on a build it does not claim. The lead generation services page is the starting point for that published offer, and the live month-to-month terms describe the term.

When a firm does publish this kind of build, keep the rest of this page's questions in play. Who handles a live reply today, not only who built the table that is supposed to route it? Where do the sending domains and mailboxes live? What do you keep if you leave after the build, including the copy and the suppression list? A workflow in your own accounts is a strong foundation. It is not, by itself, a finished outbound program, and it is not a reason to skip the checklist in the rent-or-own section.

What should you keep if you stop working with a firm?

Before you serve notice, get these four points in writing: who retains the prospect file, who retains the mail threads, who retains the CRM rows and who retains control of the sending domains. OneAway's pricing page states that a full export is available at any time and that interested leads stay yours if you leave. On Be Leaded programs, the lists and replies are yours. Ask every other firm on the list the same four points.

What is the difference between renting outbound and owning the system?

Renting means a vendor runs the motion for you, often in their tools, and the activity is hard to restart if you do not hold the domains, mailboxes, list, copy and working data. Owning means those assets live in accounts your company controls, so your staff or a later partner can keep going. Some firms sell a hybrid, with training and a written handoff. Ask who is the account owner on each tool, and get a leave plan in writing before you sign.

How should you judge an offer built around meetings?

Start with the definition. Ask whether a meeting is a booked time, a held time or a time that met a written buyer profile, and what happens if it is missed, canceled or a poor match. Ask who judges that it qualifies, how you dispute a row and whether unused meetings vanish or carry forward. Still ask the rent-or-own questions, because a full calendar is not the same as keeping the list, the domains and the copy.
