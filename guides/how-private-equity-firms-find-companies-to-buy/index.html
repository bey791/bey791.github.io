How do private equity firms find companies to buy?

By Ahmad Khan, Founder. Published and updated November 17, 2026.

Private equity firms find companies to buy through investment banks, business brokers, their own networks, portfolio company leads and direct outreach to owners who fit the fund's thesis. The firms that see the best deals work all five on purpose.

What is the short answer?

A private equity firm finds companies the way a good recruiter finds candidates. Some arrive through intermediaries who run a process. Some come from people the firm already knows. The rest the firm has to go out and find itself by deciding exactly what it wants and contacting the owners who match.

The mix matters because each channel brings different deals. Banker processes bring well prepared companies and a lot of competition. Relationships and direct outreach bring fewer finished packages but more chances to talk to an owner before anyone else does. Most funds rely on all of them in some proportion. That proportion shapes the prices they pay and the deals they win.

This guide walks through each channel, what research says about how often each one produces closed deals, how firms run their own outreach and how they decide which companies are worth chasing. It is written for deal teams at private equity funds, especially in the lower middle market where owner led companies are common.

What does research say about where private equity deals come from?

One of the clearest public sources is an academic survey. In What Do Private Equity Firms Say They Do?, Paul Gompers, Steven Kaplan and Vladimir Mukharlyamov surveyed 79 private equity investors about how they value, finance, govern and source deals. The working paper is dated April 2015 and a version was published in the Journal of Financial Economics in 2016.

The authors asked where each firm's closed deals came from. According to the working paper (PDF):

  • Almost 36% of closed deals were "proactively self-generated" by the firm.
  • 33% were generated by investment banks.
  • 8.6% came from the firm's executive network.
  • 8.6% came from deal brokers.
  • 7.4% were provided by management.
  • 4.3% came from other private equity firms.

Asked to sum it up, the investors "considered almost 48% of their closed deals to be proprietary in some way." The authors add a fair warning: "we have no way of evaluating exactly what proprietary means." The paper also notes that smaller and younger firms generally tend to source more proprietary deals.

Treat the numbers as a picture of habits rather than a current market share. The survey is a decade old and covers firms of many sizes. The pattern still holds up in conversations with deal teams today: bankers matter a great deal, yet a large share of closed deals start with the firm's own effort.

How do investment banks and sell side advisors feed deal flow?

When an owner decides to sell and hires a banker, the banker builds a list of likely buyers and runs a process. Private equity firms want to be on those lists. Getting there takes steady relationship work.

  • Be easy to understand. Bankers send teasers to firms whose criteria they can recite. A one page summary of sectors, size and deal types helps them remember you.
  • Answer fast. A firm that replies within a day with a clear yes or no earns more early looks than one that sits on teasers.
  • Close what you bid on. Bankers protect their reputations. Firms that retrade late or walk away after an exclusive period get fewer invitations.
  • Give feedback. A short note on why you passed teaches the banker what to send next time.

The tradeoff is competition. In a full auction you are compared line by line with other bidders, often including strategic buyers that can pay for synergies. Banker processes are valuable volume, but they rarely give a fund an edge on price.

What role do business brokers play for smaller funds?

Below the size where bankers usually work, business brokers handle many sales of owner led companies. For funds that buy smaller platforms or add-ons, brokers can be a useful steady source.

Brokered deals tend to involve owners who are selling for personal reasons, often retirement. The packages can be thinner than a banker's book. The owner may also have less experience with private equity terms such as rollover equity or earnouts. A fund that explains its structure clearly and treats the broker as a partner often wins on trust rather than price alone.

It also helps to understand how brokers find their sellers. Many build pipelines around succession signals such as long tenured founders with no family successor. Our guide to spotting owners nearing retirement covers those signals from the broker's side. The same signals are useful when a fund builds its own target list.

What counts as a proprietary deal?

Firms use the word loosely. At the strict end, a proprietary deal is one where the fund is the only buyer talking to the owner and no intermediary runs a process. At the loose end, it covers any deal where the fund got an early look before a wider sale.

The research above shows why the loose definition is common. When investors were asked to label their own deals, almost half were called proprietary in some way. The authors had no means to check what that meant. For your own reporting, pick a stricter test and stick to it. One practical rule is to count a deal as proprietary only if no banker or broker had started a process before your letter of intent.

Proprietary does not mean cheap. An owner who has never sold a company may still have a firm price in mind, may bring in an advisor halfway through and may take months to decide. What a proprietary approach gives you is time with the owner and a chance to shape the deal before it becomes a contest.

How does an investment thesis drive sourcing?

Every good sourcing effort starts with a written thesis that a stranger could use to find targets. Vague criteria such as "great businesses with strong management" produce vague lists. Specific criteria produce lists you can actually work.

  • Sector and subsector. Not just industrial services, but the specific services where you have conviction.
  • Size. A revenue or earnings range, with a note on what you will stretch for.
  • Geography. Where you can realistically own and support a company.
  • Ownership. Founder owned, family owned, carve outs or sponsor owned.
  • Why now. The change in the market that makes the thesis timely, such as consolidation or a shift in regulation.
  • What you bring. The specific help the fund offers beyond capital.

A tight thesis does two jobs. It tells your deal team which companies to research. It also gives every owner conversation a clear reason to exist, because you can explain exactly why you contacted that owner and not their neighbor.

How do private equity firms run direct outreach to owners?

Direct outreach is the self generated channel in practice. The firm builds a list of companies that match its thesis, finds the owner of each one and contacts them before any sale process exists. Done well, it is patient and specific. Done badly, it is a flood of generic notes that owners delete.

  • Build the universe. Pull every company that fits the thesis from databases, associations, licensing records and industry lists.
  • Qualify by hand. Remove companies that are already sponsor owned, too small, too large or clearly off thesis.
  • Find the real owner. The person who can decide on a sale, not only the highest title on the website.
  • Write a specific first note. Say what drew you to this company and how the fund would behave as a partner.
  • Follow up with care. A few spaced touches by email and LinkedIn, with a phone call where the owner has engaged.
  • Record every answer. Not now is a useful answer. Note the reason and the timing so the next touch lands at the right moment.

Email usually opens the conversation because owners can read it on their own time and pass it to an advisor. Texting an owner who never agreed to hear from you raises US consent issues, so texts rarely belong at the start. What should a fund say in a first note to an owner?

Owners of good companies hear from buyers often. The notes that get replies are short, honest about who is writing and clear about why this company.

  • Name something specific about the business, such as its niche, its history or a recent expansion.
  • Say in one line what the fund does and the kind of companies it backs.
  • Explain what a partnership could look like, such as the owner keeping a stake or staying involved.
  • Ask for something small, such as a short call to introduce the fund with no expectation of a sale.

Avoid opening with a valuation range or a request for financials. Most owners are not ready for either in a first message. Avoid language that sounds like a mass mailing. If the note could have been sent to any company, it will be treated that way.

Be clear about structure early in the conversation. Many owners have heard that private equity buys companies and then cuts staff. A plain explanation of how the fund works with management, what happens to employees and whether the owner keeps equity can do more than any pitch deck.

How do firms approach founder and family owned companies?

Most companies in the lower middle market are owned by the people who built them or by the families that inherited them. These owners often have never sold anything. Many have mixed feelings about private equity. Winning their trust takes a different posture from winning a banker's auction.

  • Lead with the people. Founders worry about their staff and customers first. Explain what happens to both before you talk about price.
  • Respect the family. A sale may involve siblings, a spouse or the next generation. Ask who else should be part of the conversation.
  • Explain the structure. Terms such as rollover equity, earnouts and board seats are unfamiliar to many owners. Plain language helps.
  • Move at their pace. Pressure to sign quickly is the fastest way to lose an owner who is still deciding.

Many funds find that the first meeting with a founder is less about the business and more about whether the owner likes the people across the table. Send the partner who would sit on the board, not only a junior associate.

How long does owner outreach take to produce a deal?

Longer than a banker process. That is the point. A banker brings a company that is ready to sell this quarter. Outreach reaches owners months or years before they would ever hire a banker.

Plan for a long horizon. A first round of outreach on a new thesis produces some early conversations, a larger group of owners who say not yet and a few who are curious but not ready. The deals tend to come from the second and third groups over time, as owners reach a decision and remember the fund that contacted them first with a thoughtful note.

That is why the record of every answer matters so much. An owner who said maybe in two years should hear from you in two years. Funds that keep that discipline turn a single campaign into a pipeline that keeps paying back long after the first notes went out.

It also means outreach should be judged on leading measures at first. Owner conversations held, owners who shared information and owners who asked to stay in touch are fair signs that the thesis and message are working while the deals take shape.

What do owners want to know before they talk to a fund?

An owner who replies to a fund usually has a short list of worries. Answering them before they are asked shortens the path to a real conversation.

  • Who are you? The fund's history, its size range and the kinds of companies it already owns.
  • What happens to my people? Whether the fund plans to keep the team, the location and the company name.
  • Do I have to leave? Whether the owner can stay on, step back slowly or hand over at closing.
  • Can I keep a piece? How rollover equity works and what a second sale later might mean for the owner.
  • Will this stay private? How the fund protects confidentiality while it learns about the company.

A one page summary that answers these points in plain words is one of the most useful tools a deal team can carry. Send it after the first reply, not in the first note, so the owner reads it when they are already curious.

How do add-on acquisitions change the way firms source?

Once a fund owns a platform company, sourcing changes. The best add-on leads often come from the platform itself. The chief executive and sales team know their competitors, their suppliers and the small firms that keep turning up in bids.

  • Ask the platform leadership for a list of companies they would buy if money were no object.
  • Use the platform's brand in outreach. An owner may be more open to joining a known peer than to selling to a fund.
  • Track local competitors that the platform loses business to or wins business from.
  • Watch suppliers and customers whose owners are nearing retirement.

Add-on outreach can be more targeted than platform outreach because the buyer is a real company in the same industry. Owners can visit, meet the team and see what life would look like after the sale. That makes a first conversation far less abstract.

How do executive networks and advisors bring in deals?

The research above shows executive networks and management teams as smaller channels than banks or self generated deals, yet they often bring the deals a fund knows best. Executives who have run companies in your sector hear about owners thinking of a sale. Accountants, lawyers and lenders who serve owner led companies hear even earlier.

Building that network is slow work. Keep a short list of executives you would back in your target sectors and stay in touch with them. Meet the regional accounting and law firms that serve founder owned companies. Give them a clear sense of what you buy and how you treat owners and their advisors during a deal.

Reward introductions properly and visibly. An advisor who sees you treat a referred client well will send another. An advisor who feels cut out of a deal will not.

How do data platforms and events help sourcing?

Company databases make it possible to build a thesis list in days instead of months. They are a starting point, not an answer. Data on private companies is often out of date. The person listed as the decision maker is not always the owner. Plan to check every target by hand before contacting anyone.

Industry events are a different kind of source. Trade shows and association meetings put you in a room with owners who would never answer an email from a fund. The aim is not to pitch on the floor but to meet people, learn the industry and follow up later.

Deal marketplaces and online listing platforms sit somewhere between these channels and the banker process. They can surface smaller companies quickly, but they are usually visible to many buyers at once.

How many companies does a firm look at to close one deal?

Many. The same NBER survey asked about the deal funnel. In the authors' words, "for every hundred opportunities, considered, the average PE investor deeply investigates fewer than 24, signs an agreement with fewer than 14 and closes on only 6."

That ratio explains why sourcing is a volume game even for selective funds. If you want a few closed deals a year, you need a large and steady flow of first looks. It also explains why outreach should be measured by early steps such as owner conversations rather than by closed deals alone, which arrive slowly and rarely.

The authors also report that larger and older firms pass a greater fraction of deals through to the next stage. One explanation they give is that those firms may have higher quality initial sourcing, so they need to screen out fewer deals at each step.

How do firms decide which companies are worth pursuing?

Screening starts long before financials arrive. Most deal teams run a quick filter on every new name and then a deeper look at the few that pass.

  • Thesis fit. Does the company sit squarely in the sector, size and geography you wrote down?
  • Owner situation. Is there a reason the owner might engage now, such as retirement, a partner leaving or a need for growth capital?
  • Business quality. Recurring revenue, customer spread and a team that can run the company beyond the founder.
  • Your edge. Do you have a real reason to win, such as sector knowledge, an add-on angle or a relationship?

Score names quickly and revisit them often. A company that fails the owner situation test today may pass it next year when the founder's plans change.

How should a fund measure its sourcing?

Track sourcing as a pipeline with clear stages and dates. The aim is to see which channels produce conversations that turn into real deals, not just which channels produce the most noise.

  • New companies added to the thesis list, by source.
  • First conversations with owners or advisors.
  • Companies that shared information or met in person.
  • Indications of interest and letters of intent, by source.
  • Closed deals, by source and by how proprietary the firm judged them.

Review the numbers each quarter. If banker processes produce many looks but few wins, it may be time to put more effort into owner outreach. If outreach produces conversations but no letters of intent, the thesis or the message may need work.

What mistakes slow private equity sourcing down?

  • A thesis that is too broad. It produces lists too large to work and notes too generic to answer.
  • Waiting for teasers. A fund that relies only on banker processes sees what every other fund sees.
  • Generic outreach. Owners can tell a mass mailing in one line.
  • Slow replies. Bankers, brokers and owners all move on when a fund takes a week to respond.
  • No follow up system. An owner who said not yet last spring is forgotten by fall.
  • Treating owners like sellers on day one. Most are not sellers yet. They become sellers when they trust you.

Each of these has the same fix: write down what you want, contact the right owners on purpose and keep a careful record of every answer.

Frequently asked questions

How do private equity firms find companies to buy?

Through investment banks and sell side advisors, business brokers, executive and advisor networks, leads from portfolio companies and direct outreach to owners whose businesses fit the fund's thesis.

What share of private equity deals are proprietary?

In a survey of 79 private equity investors published by NBER, the investors considered almost 48% of their closed deals proprietary in some way. The authors note they could not check what proprietary meant to each firm.

Do most private equity deals come from investment banks?

Banks are a major source. In the same survey, 33% of closed deals were generated by investment banks, while almost 36% were proactively self generated by the firms.

How many companies does a fund review to close one deal?

The NBER survey found that for every hundred opportunities considered, the average investor deeply investigates fewer than 24, signs an agreement with fewer than 14 and closes on only 6.

What is direct owner outreach in private equity?

It is contacting owners of companies that fit the fund's thesis before any sale process exists, usually by email first with follow ups by LinkedIn and phone.

How do funds find add-on acquisitions?

Often through the platform company itself. Its leaders know competitors, suppliers and small firms in the market. Outreach can also use the platform's name.

Should a fund text owners it has never met?

Usually not at the start. In the US, texting someone who has not agreed to it raises consent issues. A short email is the more natural opening.

Does Be Leaded advise on deals or raise capital?

No. Be Leaded contacts owners in the fund's name and books conversations. Valuation, diligence, terms and capital stay with the fund and its advisors.
