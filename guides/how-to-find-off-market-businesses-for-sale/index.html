What off-market means when you are the buyer

A business is off-market when no one is running a sale for it. There is no broker listing, no banker teaser and no data room. The owner may never have thought about selling. Or the owner may have thought about it privately and told nobody.

For a buyer that changes the work. With a listed company the seller comes to you with a package. With an off-market company you have to find it, learn enough to judge it from the outside and work out who can decide on a sale. This guide covers that finding and checking. It does not assume an owner wants to sell. Nothing in the public records below tells you that either.

Some companies sit in between. An owner may have spoken to one adviser or turned down an approach last year. Treat those as off-market too, but expect that someone else may already know about them.

Who this method suits

The steps are the same for most buyers. What changes is the size band, how many companies you need to look at and how much time you can give the work each week.

  • Searchers who want a single company to buy and lead themselves.
  • Independent sponsors who find a deal first and raise equity for it after.
  • Private equity funds looking for platforms or add-ons.
  • Strategic acquirers buying competitors, suppliers or companies in a new region.

Short notes for each buyer type come later in the page. The core method comes first because it applies to all of them.

Write the buy box before you search

A buy box is a written description of the company you would buy. Without one, every source looks useful and the list grows without getting better. Write it down before you open a single database. Keep it to one page.

  • Industry. Name the trades or services in plain words, then the ones you will not consider even if they look close.
  • Region. The states, metro areas or distance from home base you can cover.
  • Size. A band you can afford and manage, written as staff count or number of sites if you cannot see revenue from outside.
  • Ownership. Founder or family owned, with no outside investor, unless you are open to buying from another fund.
  • Deal shape. A full purchase, a majority stake or an add-on to a company you already own.
  • Deal breakers. Heavy reliance on one customer, a license you cannot hold or a model you do not understand.

Share the buy box with anyone who will help you search. It is the test every company on the list has to pass.

Turn the buy box into search terms

Public records and data sets do not understand plain descriptions. They sort companies by codes, keywords and license types. Translating the buy box into those terms is what makes the later steps fast.

  • Industry codes. Find the NAICS codes that match your target trades. Note neighbors that catch the same companies under a different label.
  • Trade words. List the words a company in the niche would use on its own website, including slang and service names.
  • License types. If the trade is licensed, note the exact license names in each state you cover.
  • Exclusion words. Terms that signal a company you do not want, such as a franchise brand or a retail format.

Keep this list next to the buy box. When a search returns too much noise, the fix is usually a better code or a sharper exclusion word rather than more hours of manual sorting.

The main places off-market targets come from

No single source holds every company you might buy. Good lists are built by layering several sources, each of which catches companies the others miss.

  • State business registers, for whether a company exists and is in good standing.
  • Federal data sets, for companies that took certain loans, run benefit plans or do federal work.
  • Licensing boards in regulated trades.
  • Trade associations, show exhibitor lists and industry directories.
  • Company databases bought or licensed for list building.
  • People: brokers, lenders, accountants, lawyers and owners you already know.

The sections below take each in turn. Record which source a company came from. Later you will want to know which sources produced companies worth keeping.

State business registers

Every state keeps a register of companies formed or registered to do business there. Start here to confirm that a company on the list is real and active. It also shows the exact legal name, which often differs from the name on the truck or the website.

What a record shows varies by state, so read the field list on that state's own site before you rely on it. Use the register to confirm status and the legal entity. Do not treat an officer name on a filing as proof of who owns the company. Filings are made for legal reasons and often name an accountant, a lawyer or a family member.

If you cover several states, search each one separately. A company formed in one state and working in another can show up in both.

SBA loan records

The Small Business Administration publishes loan records for its 7(a) and 504 programs as downloadable files. Each record is a loan: the borrower's name and address, the lender, the amount and the approval date. SBA's data dictionary lists every field. Its page says the files are updated each quarter.

For a buyer, these records are a way to find companies by industry and place that a bank has lent to. They are not a measure of size and they say nothing about who owns the company today or whether anyone would sell. A loan approved years ago may belong to a company that has since closed or changed hands.

SBA also publishes records for the Paycheck Protection Program. Those loans were made in 2020 and 2021, so treat that data as old. It can help you find names in a niche, but check every company against a current source before it goes on your list.

  • SBA: 7(a) and 504 loan data (official page, opened Nov 19, 2026)
  • SBA: Paycheck Protection Program loan data (official page, opened Nov 19, 2026)

Form 5500 benefit plan filings

Employee benefit plans file an annual return on Form 5500 with the Department of Labor. The department offers a public search of those filings. Only companies that sponsor such a plan appear, so many small businesses will not be there at all.

When a target does appear, a filing tells you the company sponsors a plan. Treat that as one fact about the company, then go back to your other sources for everything else. Do not read a filing as evidence of revenue, profit or owner intent.

  • Department of Labor: Form 5500 series (official page, opened Nov 19, 2026)
  • Department of Labor: Form 5500 filing search (official page, opened Nov 19, 2026)

Federal contractor registrations

SAM.gov is where entities register to do business with the federal government. Its entity search lets you view entities doing federal business. It covers only federal contractors and grantees. If your buy box includes companies that sell to federal agencies, it can help you find and confirm them.

Companies that never sell to the government have no reason to register, so leave SAM.gov out entirely if your targets sell only to consumers or private businesses. When a company does appear, treat its registration as one fact about its customers rather than a profile of the business.

  • SAM.gov: entity registration and search (official site, opened Nov 19, 2026)

Licensing boards and trade registers

In licensed trades, state boards keep lists of who holds a license. Contractors, electricians, plumbers, pest control, home health and many other services work under one. A board lookup can confirm that a company holds the license it needs and show the name the license is held under.

Each board runs its own site and its own rules, so check what that board publishes before you build a process around it. Some boards offer full downloads. Others allow only one search at a time.

Licensing data is especially useful for the deal breaker line in your buy box. If you cannot hold a required license yourself, a company whose license sits with one person tells you to plan for that early.

Trade associations, exhibitor lists and directories

Associations publish member directories. Trade shows publish exhibitor lists. Industry magazines run supplier guides. These sources tend to catch the established companies in a niche, because joining and exhibiting cost money and time.

Treat a directory as a starting list rather than a finished one. Listings go stale and many members are suppliers rather than the companies you want to buy. Use your exclusion words to clear out the noise, then check what remains against a state register.

Going to a show in person is a separate tactic. It lets you learn the industry and meet people without pitching anyone. Note who you met and add them to the list with that context.

Company databases and their limits

Paid company databases can give you thousands of names in an afternoon. They are useful for a first pull across a wide region, especially when you already know your codes and trade words.

Their weakness is small private companies. Staff counts are often estimates, contact details go stale and the person listed as the top contact is not always the owner. Expect to correct or remove a large share of what you pull. Budget the time for it.

Use databases for breadth and public records for checking. A company that appears in a database but not in any state register needs a closer look before it stays on the list.

Brokers, lenders and your own network

Not every off-market lead comes from data. Brokers sometimes know of owners who are not ready to list. Bankers and lenders know which local companies are doing well. Accountants and lawyers work with owners every week. Owners you already know can introduce you to others in the same trade.

Treat these people as a source that runs beside your list. Tell them your buy box in one or two sentences and ask them to keep you in mind. Add any company they mention to the list with a note of who mentioned it, then run the same checks as any other name.

Funds run this at a larger scale, with banker coverage and deal teams.

Owner checks: is the company independent?

Before a company stays on the list, check that it is still the kind of company in your buy box. Most of that is about who controls it today.

  • Outside investors. Look for a fund named on the website, a press release about an investment or a parent company in the footer.
  • Part of a group. A local name can belong to a larger company that bought it. Check the legal entity in the state register against the brand.
  • Franchise units. If franchises are out of your buy box, check the website and filings for a franchise brand.
  • Recent sale. News of a sale in the last few years usually means the new owner is not looking to sell again soon.

No public source settles who owns a private company. Each check is a clue. Keep the company if the clues point to an independent owner and confirm the rest when you speak with them.

Size checks without financials

Off-market companies do not publish accounts, so size has to be estimated from the outside. Use several clues and write down which ones you used, so the estimate can be corrected later.

  • Staff shown on the website or on professional profiles.
  • Number of locations, service areas or vehicles.
  • Job postings and how often they appear.
  • The scale of projects or customers named on the website.

Label every size figure on your list as an estimate. A company slightly outside your band on paper may fit once you see real numbers. One that looks right may turn out much smaller. The point at this stage is to remove clear misfits, not to value the business.

Find the owner's name and a working address

A company on the list needs a person attached to it. For most small private companies that is the founder or the family member who runs it day to day. Start with the company's own about page, then professional profiles, local news and association listings.

Next, find at least one way to reach that person that actually works. A mailing address for the business, a verified email address or an active professional profile are all usable. Verify emails before anything is sent, because a list full of bounces tells you little and harms your sending.

If you cannot name a likely owner after a reasonable search, mark the company as unresolved rather than guessing. Choosing who exactly to write to and how belongs to the approach stage.

Build the target list

A target list is a working file, not a report. Keep one row per company and the same fields for every row, so the list can be sorted, filtered and shared.

  • Company legal name and trading name.
  • Website, city and state.
  • Industry code and your own one-line description.
  • Source the company came from.
  • Size estimate and the clues behind it.
  • Owner name and how confident you are in it.
  • Best contact route and whether it is verified.
  • Independence check result.
  • Fit score and the date of the last check.
  • Notes and the next step.

A shared spreadsheet is enough to start. Move to a CRM once outreach begins, so contact history sits next to the company record.

Score the list for fit

Score each company on how well it matches the buy box, not on how likely the owner is to sell. A simple three-point scale works: strong fit, possible fit and weak fit. Score industry, region, size and independence separately, then combine them.

Keep the scoring rules written down so two people looking at one company reach the same score. Rescore when you learn something new, such as a better size estimate or news of an outside investor.

Readiness is a separate question. A post on our blog sets out the readiness clues advisors look for when they decide which owners to contact first. Keep any readiness notes in their own column so they never change the fit score.

Work the list in batches

Research the list in batches you can finish properly, then move on. A batch is a set of companies that you can check, score and name an owner for within a week or two at your own pace.

Batches stop the list from drifting. Every company in a finished batch has the same fields filled in and the same checks done, which makes outreach easier to plan and replies easier to read.

Start each batch with your strongest source for that niche, then fill gaps from the others. When a source keeps producing weak fits, use it less in the next batch.

Keep the list clean

A list that is not maintained gets worse every month. Companies close, owners change, addresses stop working and people ask not to be contacted.

  • Remove duplicates by legal name and website, since one company can come in from several sources.
  • Keep a do-not-contact list and check every new batch against it.
  • Date every check so you know which rows are getting old.
  • Recheck before outreach if a company was researched more than a few months earlier.
  • Record why a company was dropped, so it does not come back in the next pull.

For a broader sense of how crowded a market is before you commit to it, the Census Bureau publishes Statistics of U.S. Businesses.

A worked example from buy box to first batch

Here is how the method runs for one illustrative buy box. The details are made up to show the steps. They are not a record of a real search.

A buyer wants a commercial HVAC service company in two neighboring states, independent and owner-run, with enough staff that the owner is no longer on every job. The buy box rules out residential-only firms, franchise units and anything with an outside investor.

The buyer maps that to the NAICS code that covers plumbing, heating and air conditioning contractors, which means plumbing firms will need filtering out. The buyer adds trade words such as rooftop units, chillers and preventive maintenance contracts and notes the mechanical contractor license name in each state. Exclusion words include residential, home comfort and the names of franchise brands.

The first pull comes from the two state licensing boards and a trade association directory, then SBA loan records filtered by the same code and states. Each company is checked against the state business register. Websites are read for outside investors and franchise branding. Size is estimated from staff pages, vehicles and service areas. Every estimate is labeled.

The first batch ends with each company scored strong, possible or weak, an owner named where one could be found and unresolved rows marked as such. Only the strong and possible fits with a verified contact route move on to outreach. Everything else stays on the list with a note on what is missing.

Notes for searchers

A searcher usually needs one company and has limited time to look. Keep the buy box tight, research a smaller number of companies more deeply and check early whether each one could support the financing you plan to use.

Searchers also tend to be the person who will run the company, so personal fit belongs in the buy box. A smaller, carefully checked list usually suits a search better than a long one. If you want the outreach handled while you keep searching, see our owner outreach for searchers.

Notes for independent sponsors

An independent sponsor finds a deal before raising equity for it, so the list has to show capital partners that the company fits a clear plan. Keep the scoring notes and the size clues tidy enough to share.

Sponsors often cover a wider range of industries than searchers. Write a separate buy box for each one rather than one broad version.

Notes for private equity funds and add-ons

A fund working on add-ons has an advantage: the platform company already knows the market. Ask its leadership which competitors and suppliers they respect, then put those names through the same checks as any other row.

Platform buyers often care about region and service mix more than size. Build the buy box around what the platform lacks, such as a new territory or a service it does not offer yet. Keep add-on lists separate from new platform lists so the scoring stays honest.

Notes for strategic acquirers

A company buying in its own industry knows the field better than any outside buyer. Its sales team often knows which competitors are strong in which regions. Its suppliers know which customers are growing.

The risk for a strategic buyer is talking about acquisitions too openly. Keep the list inside a small group and treat it as confidential. Use the same owner checks as anyone else, because a familiar competitor may have taken on an outside investor without much notice.

Reaching owners once the list is ready

When a batch is finished, decide how each owner will first hear from you. The route depends on the data you hold. A business address supports a letter. A verified email supports a short email. An active professional profile supports a connection request with a note.

Before anything goes out, check the batch one last time against your do-not-contact list and remove anything unresolved. Any outreach has to follow email and privacy law where you and the owner are.

What to say, how to follow up and how to handle the first call are covered in our guide on how to approach the owner.

What responses tell you about your list

Replies are feedback on your list as much as on your message. Sort them by what they say about the data, then fix the list before the next batch.

  • Bounced or returned mail. The contact route was stale. Recheck how you found it.
  • Wrong person. Your owner check missed. Note where the name came from.
  • Already sold or has an investor. Your independence check missed. Add the clue you overlooked to the checklist.
  • Too small or too large. Your size clues were off. Adjust the band or the clues.
  • Not now. The company may still fit. Keep it with a date to look again.
  • Already working with an adviser. Note it and respect the process the owner has chosen.

Track these by source. Over a few batches you will see which sources give you clean data for your niche and which create rework.

Mistakes that weaken an off-market list

  • Starting to search before the buy box is written.
  • Keeping every name a database returns without checking it.
  • Treating an officer on a filing as the owner.
  • Using old loan records without a current check.
  • Mixing fit and readiness into one score.
  • Letting the list age between research and outreach.
  • Failing to record why a company was dropped.

Each one adds work later. Most are fixed by writing rules down and applying them the same way to every row.

Doing it yourself or hiring it out

Building and checking a list is steady, careful work. Many buyers do it themselves, especially early in a search or when the niche is small. It gets harder when the list runs to hundreds of companies and the same person also has to raise money, review deals and take calls.

Frequently asked questions

How do I find off-market businesses to buy?

Write a buy box, turn it into codes and search terms, pull companies from several public and paid sources, check each one is independent and in your size band, name the owner and keep the results in one scored list.

Where do off-market companies come from?

From state business registers, federal data sets such as SBA loan records, benefit plan filings and federal contractor registrations, licensing boards, trade associations, company databases and people who know the industry.

Do public records show who owns a private company?

Not reliably. Filings exist for legal reasons and may name an accountant, lawyer or relative. Treat each record as a clue and confirm ownership when you speak with the owner.

Is PPP loan data still useful?

Only as a way to find names. PPP loans were made in 2020 and 2021, so the data is old. Check every company against a current source before it goes on your list.

How many companies should be on my list?

There is no right number. Work in batches you can research properly, with the same fields and checks for every company. Add more batches as you learn which sources work.

Should I score companies on how likely they are to sell?

Score fit and readiness separately. Fit is how well a company matches your buy box. Readiness is a guess about the owner, so keep it in its own column.
