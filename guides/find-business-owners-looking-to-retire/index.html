How to find business owners looking to retire

By Ahmad Khan, Founder. Published and updated November 17, 2026.

To find business owners looking to retire, track signals you can see: decades at the top, no visible successor and an owner stepping back from daily work. Then reach them early through their advisors and through outreach about succession, not a sale.

What does an owner looking to retire look like from the outside?

Almost nobody announces it. An owner who is thinking about retirement rarely posts about it, rarely tells staff and often has not said it out loud to a spouse yet. What you can see instead is a pattern. The founder has run the company for a long time. The people who might take over are not obvious. The owner is spending fewer hours in the building and more time on things outside it. Growth spending slows while the business stays healthy.

No single clue proves anything. A sixty year old founder may plan to work until eighty. A forty five year old owner may be burned out and ready to leave next year. The skill is in stacking several weak signals until a short list of owners stands out, then starting a conversation that respects how private the decision is.

This guide is for brokers and M&A advisors building a pipeline of retirement driven sell side mandates. It covers the signals worth tracking, where to find them, how to rank a list and how to open a first conversation without spooking the owner. If you want the wider picture of every reason an owner might sell, including partner disputes, health and outside offers, read our broader guide to spotting owners who might sell. This page stays on retirement and succession.

Why does retirement matter so much to sell side pipelines?

Retirement is the exit trigger you can plan around. Distress, divorce and disputes arrive without warning. Retirement builds slowly over years, which means a broker who is patient can be the first trusted name in the room when the owner finally decides.

Survey data points the same way. In a July 2026 article on its blog, the Exit Planning Institute cites its 2023 National State of Owner Readiness Report. It says 75 percent of business owners intend to exit within the next ten years, yet only 42 percent have a formal written transition plan. It also says 78 percent have not assembled an exit planning team.

Read that gap carefully. Most owners in that survey expect to leave within a decade, yet fewer than half have a plan on paper and most have no advisory team around them. That is the opening for a broker. The owner who has not built a team yet is still deciding who to trust.

The same 2023 report (PDF) notes its own sample limits. It was built from 1,162 owner responses gathered with research partners. Treat the figures as a guide to the pattern rather than a count of owners in your territory.

Which signals suggest an owner is thinking about retirement?

Group the signals into three families: time, people and behavior. Owners who show signs in two or three families deserve a closer look. Owners who show one sign belong on a watch list.

Time signals

  • The company was founded twenty five years ago or more and the founder is still the top name on the website.
  • The owner's first listed role on a professional profile dates back several decades.
  • Trade association pages or local press mark a big company anniversary with the founder pictured alone.
  • State filings show the same officer on the annual report year after year with no new names added.

People signals

  • No second generation family member appears in a leadership role.
  • Adult children have built careers in unrelated fields.
  • A general manager or president was hired from outside in the last couple of years.
  • A long serving partner or cofounder has already left.

Behavior signals

  • The owner has handed off customer relationships they used to keep for themselves.
  • Big capital projects have stopped while the business keeps running smoothly.
  • The owner is spending more time on boards, charities or a second home.
  • The company has started documenting processes that used to live only in the owner's head.

The last signal is worth a note. When a founder starts writing things down, the business is becoming transferable. Owners rarely do that work by accident. It is often the first quiet step toward handing the keys to someone else.

Where can you see owner tenure without guessing?

You do not need private data to estimate how long an owner has been in charge. Most of the evidence sits in public view. The work is in collecting it the same way for every company so your list stays fair.

  • State business registries. Formation dates tell you how old the entity is. Annual reports show officers and how rarely that list changes.
  • Company websites. About pages, history timelines and team pages show who founded the company and who runs it today.
  • Professional profiles. Career start dates and job history give you a rough sense of how long the owner has worked.
  • Local business press. Anniversary stories, awards and profile pieces often mention succession in passing.
  • Trade associations. Member directories list long standing firms. Past officer lists show who has been active in the industry for years.
  • Licensing boards. Contractors, engineers and other licensed trades often show the original license date of the qualifying owner.

Record what you found and where you found it. A note like "founded 1994, founder still president, no other officers since 2010" is far more useful six months from now than a vague tag that says retiring.

Stay away from anything that feels like snooping. Health details, family conflict and gossip from former staff do not belong in a prospect file. They are unreliable and the owner will feel it if you bring them up.

Which succession signals matter more than age?

Age is the easiest signal to estimate and the weakest on its own. Plenty of founders in their seventies have no intention of leaving. What usually moves an owner from someday to soon is the succession picture around them.

Ask three questions about every company on your list. Is there an obvious internal successor? Is there a family member who wants the business? Has the owner started building the bench that would let them step away? If the honest answer to the first two is no and the third is yes, you are probably looking at an owner who has started thinking about a third party sale even if they have not said so.

Watch for the outside hire. When a founder led company brings in a general manager or chief operating officer from outside after decades of the founder doing everything, it often signals one of two plans. Either the owner wants to step back and keep the company. Or the owner wants to make it sellable. Both are worth a conversation. The second is exactly where a broker earns a mandate.

Watch for family drift too. A son who became a doctor or a daughter who runs her own firm in another city tells you the family path is probably closed. Owners in that position often feel they have to choose between closing the doors and selling to a stranger. Many do not know what a sale to a stranger involves.

Which advisors hear about retirement plans first?

Owners talk to their money people long before they talk to a broker. A relationship with those advisors is the slow, steady channel that sits beside any outreach you run yourself.

  • Accountants. The outside CPA sees the numbers every year and often hears the owner ask what the business might be worth.
  • Estate attorneys. Estate plans force the question of what happens to the company.
  • Wealth managers. A financial plan built around retirement needs an answer for the largest asset the owner holds.
  • Commercial bankers. They notice when an owner stops borrowing to grow or asks about paying down debt early.
  • Insurance brokers. Key person and buy sell coverage reviews bring up succession directly.
  • Exit planners. Some owners hire them years before any sale. They are natural referral partners for a broker who treats their clients well.

The best referral relationships run both ways. Send these advisors useful work, keep them informed when you are talking to one of their clients and never cut them out of a deal. An accountant who trusts you will mention your name at exactly the moment an owner asks what comes next.

How do you start a referral relationship with an owner's accountant?

Approach an accountant or estate attorney the way you would want a stranger to approach you. Lead with what you can do for their clients, not with a request for names.

A good first meeting covers three things. Explain the kind of companies you sell and the size range you work in. Describe how you treat the client's existing advisors during a sale, including keeping them involved in tax and estate questions. Then offer something concrete, such as a short session for their clients on what buyers look for in an owner led company.

Do not expect referrals right away. Most advisors wait until they have watched you handle one client well. Stay in touch, share what you learn about buyer demand in their clients' industries and make introductions back to them when an owner you meet needs tax or estate help.

Track these relationships in the same system as your owner list. An accountant with dozens of business clients in your target industries can be worth more over five years than any single prospect.

How do you build a retirement signal list for one territory?

Start small and specific. A list of every company in a state is useless. A list of two hundred owner led firms in three industries you know well is a pipeline.

  • Pick the box. Choose the industries, revenue band and geography where you have closed deals or can credibly sell one.
  • Pull the universe. Use a company database, association directories and state registries to list every firm in that box.
  • Remove the obvious misfits. Drop companies owned by private equity, public companies, franchise units and firms already listed for sale.
  • Find the owner. For each remaining company, identify the person who actually owns it, not just the most senior title on the website.
  • Collect the signals. Record founding year, owner tenure, visible successors, family involvement and any recent leadership hires.
  • Note the source. Every signal gets a source and a date so you can check it again later.
  • Score and tier. Use the simple scoring in the next section to sort the list into three groups.

Expect the research to take longer than the outreach. That is normal. A good retirement list is built slowly and then used for years, with each owner checked again every few months.

How should you score and rank the list?

Keep scoring simple enough that anyone on your team would score the same company the same way. Complex models feel precise and usually hide guesswork.

One workable approach gives each company a point for every signal family where you found real evidence. Time, people and behavior give you a score from zero to three. Then add one point if the company sits squarely in your best industry and size range.

  • Tier one (three or four points). Reach out now with a personal note and plan a follow up.
  • Tier two (two points). Reach out with lighter touch outreach and check signals again in three months.
  • Tier three (zero or one point). Keep on a watch list and look for new signals each quarter.

Review the tiers after every few conversations. If owners you rated tier one keep telling you they have no plans, your signals are off. If tier three owners keep replying with interest, something you are not tracking matters more than you thought.

What should you learn about one owner before reaching out?

A tier one owner deserves ten minutes of real reading before any message goes out. The goal is not a dossier. It is enough context to write a note that could only have been sent to that one person.

  • The company story. When it started, what it sells today and how that has changed over the years.
  • The owner's role now. Whether they still sign every quote or have stepped back to the strategy and the bank relationship.
  • The people around them. Who else is named on the team page and how long those people have been there.
  • Recent change. A new location, a sold building, a new product line or a leadership hire in the past year or two.
  • Public voice. Interviews, talks at association meetings or posts where the owner explains what they care about.

Write down one sentence about why you think this owner may be thinking about the future. If you cannot write that sentence from the evidence you found, the owner probably belongs in a lower tier for now.

Keep the research respectful. Owners can tell when a note was written by someone who read their company history. They can also tell when someone has been digging into their private life. That ends the conversation before it starts.

What makes a retiring owner's business easier to sell?

Brokers win retirement mandates by helping owners see their company the way a buyer will. Many founders assume the business sells itself because it has been profitable for years. Buyers look at different things.

  • Less dependence on the owner. A buyer wants to know the customers, the suppliers and the staff will stay once the founder leaves.
  • A second layer of leaders. A general manager or a few strong department heads make the transition far less risky.
  • Clean records. Clear financial statements and separated personal expenses shorten diligence and build trust.
  • Written processes. Pricing rules, quoting steps and service routines that live on paper rather than in one person's memory.
  • A spread of customers. A company where no single client dominates the revenue is easier to finance and easier to buy.

This list is also something useful you can offer in a first conversation. An owner who is two or three years away from retirement often appreciates a plain explanation of what to work on now. That advice costs you nothing and positions you as the person who understood the business before anyone else did.

Be honest when the gaps are big. An owner who still does every sale personally may need time before going to market. Saying so earns more trust than talking up a quick close you cannot deliver.

How do you track retirement signals over time?

Retirement pipelines live or die on memory. A signal you spotted in March is only useful if someone sees it again in September. Put the list in a system your whole firm uses and keep the fields simple.

  • Company, owner name and how you confirmed the owner.
  • Founding year and estimated owner tenure with the source for each.
  • Succession notes, such as family involvement or an outside leadership hire.
  • Tier, the date it was set and the reason.
  • Every touch: date, channel, what you said and what the owner said back.
  • The next planned check and who owns it.

Set a recurring review. Once a quarter, walk through tier one and tier two, check for new signals and decide who gets a fresh note. Owners move between stages quietly. A regular review is how you notice before a competitor does.

How should you write the first note to a retiring owner?

Never lead with a sale. An owner who has not decided anything will close the door on a stranger who asks whether the business is for sale. Lead with succession, timing and options instead. Those are the topics the owner is already turning over in private.

A good first note is short, specific to the company and easy to answer. It shows you understand the industry and it gives the owner a reason to reply that does not commit them to anything.

Here is the shape of a note that tends to read well:

  • One line that shows you know the company, such as its founding year or a recent milestone.
  • One line about the question many founders in that industry are weighing, such as what happens to the company and its people when they step back.
  • One line that explains who you are and the kind of owners you help.
  • A small ask, such as whether a short, confidential call about options would be useful this year or later.

Leave out valuations, multiples and buyer names in the first note. They make the message feel like a pitch. Save them for the conversation, when the owner has asked.

Confidentiality matters more with retiring owners than almost anyone else. Staff, customers and suppliers who hear rumors of a sale can damage the business the owner spent decades building. Say plainly that the conversation stays private, then behave that way.

What should you ask on the first call?

The first call is not an intake form. It is the owner deciding whether you are someone they could trust with the biggest financial decision of their life. Ask open questions and listen more than you talk.

  • What would you want your life to look like five years from now?
  • Who runs the business on a day when you are not there?
  • Have you talked about the future of the company with your family?
  • Who else advises you today, such as an accountant or attorney?
  • What matters most to you about the people and customers after you step back?
  • What would need to be true for you to feel ready?

Notice what the owner cares about. Some care most about price. Many care just as much about keeping staff employed, keeping the company name or staying on for a while. Those answers shape which buyers fit and how you would run the process if the owner hires you.

End with a clear next step, even if it is small. A follow up in three months, an introduction to an exit planner or an informal look at what the business might be worth can all keep the relationship moving.

How long does it take a retiring owner to come to market?

Longer than most brokers would like. Owners who are thinking about retirement tend to move through stages. Each stage can take months or years.

  • Thinking. The owner knows the day is coming but has not decided how or when.
  • Planning. The owner starts asking advisors questions and may begin cleaning up the books or the leadership team.
  • Ready. The owner has a rough date, a sense of what they need from a sale and is choosing who to work with.

Your outreach should match the stage. An owner who is thinking needs light, useful contact and no pressure. An owner who is planning needs practical help such as introductions and information about how a sale works. An owner who is ready needs a clear proposal and a broker who has already earned trust.

This is why early contact pays off. The broker who reaches an owner in the thinking stage and stays in touch with care is often the one the owner calls when they reach the ready stage.

How do you stay in touch with owners who say not yet?

Not yet is the most common good answer you will hear. Treat it as a door that is open a crack, not a rejection. The goal is to stay useful and present without becoming a nuisance.

  • Ask permission to check in and agree on a rough timing, such as next spring.
  • Send something useful now and then, such as a note on what buyers are asking about in their industry.
  • Mark personal details the owner shared, such as a planned move or a new grandchild. Remember them.
  • Invite them to small, private events with other owners rather than big public seminars.
  • Revisit the signals on their company every few months and reach out when something changes.

Keep notes in one place so anyone in your firm can pick up the relationship. Owners notice when a broker remembers what they said last year. They also notice when three different people from the same firm send them the same note.

Which outreach channels work best with older owners?

Use the channels the owner already uses for business. That often means email and the phone first, with professional networks and printed letters as useful extras.

  • Email. Easy to read on the owner's schedule and easy to forward to a spouse or accountant.
  • LinkedIn. Useful when the owner is active there. Many founders of older companies are not.
  • Phone. Works best after a written note so the call is not a cold surprise.
  • Printed letters. A short, well made letter can stand out with owners who get little paper mail from strangers.
  • Events. Small dinners or round tables with peers build trust faster than any message.

Text messages are a poor first touch for a stranger and carry consent rules in the US. If you are weighing texting against email for owner outreach, our comparison of cold SMS and cold email walks through where each one belongs and why email should usually open the conversation.

What mistakes do brokers make with retiring owners?

The same few mistakes cost brokers retirement mandates again and again.

  • Asking if the business is for sale. It forces a no from owners who have not decided.
  • Quoting a multiple too early. It anchors the owner on a number before you know the business.
  • Treating age as the only signal. It fills your list with founders who will never leave.
  • Ignoring the owner's advisors. The accountant or attorney who feels bypassed can quietly steer the owner elsewhere.
  • Giving up after one reply. A not yet this year can become a mandate in two years if you stay in touch.
  • Breaking confidence. Mentioning one owner's plans to another in the same town ends both relationships.

Most of these come from impatience. Retirement driven deals reward brokers who are willing to play a long, quiet game.

Can buyers use the same retirement signals?

Yes. Private equity firms, search funds and strategic acquirers watch the same signals because a retiring founder with no successor is one of the most common sources of off market deals. The difference is in how they approach the owner. A buyer is pitching one offer. A broker is offering a process and a choice of buyers.

That difference is a real advantage for brokers. Many owners feel uneasy talking to one buyer directly because they do not know whether the offer is fair. A broker who can explain the options and run a confidential process is often the more comfortable first conversation.

Frequently asked questions

How do you find business owners looking to retire?

Track signals you can see in public: long tenure at the top, no visible successor, family members in other careers and an owner who is stepping back from daily work. Then reach those owners through their advisors and through direct outreach that talks about succession, not a sale.

What are the signs a business owner is ready to retire?

The strongest signs come in combinations: decades in charge, no internal or family successor, a recent outside leadership hire, slowing growth spending and an owner who is handing off customer relationships.

Is owner age a reliable signal on its own?

No. Age is easy to estimate but many older founders have no plan to leave. Succession signals such as a missing successor or a new general manager usually tell you more.

Should the first message mention selling the business?

No. Lead with succession, timing and options. Asking whether a business is for sale forces a no from owners who have not decided yet.

How long does it take a retiring owner to decide to sell?

It varies widely. Many owners move from thinking to planning to ready over months or years, which is why early and patient contact pays off.

Which advisors hear about retirement plans first?

Accountants, estate attorneys, wealth managers, commercial bankers, insurance brokers and exit planners often hear about retirement plans long before a broker does.

Is texting a good way to reach retiring owners?

Rarely as a first touch. Text messages to strangers carry consent rules in the US. Email or a letter usually opens the conversation better.

No. Be Leaded writes to owners on your behalf under your firm's name and books conversations. Valuation, the engagement and the sale stay with your firm.
