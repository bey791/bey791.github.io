B2B paid social agency vs in-house: how to decide

By Ahmad Khan, Founder. Published and updated November 24, 2026.

The B2B paid social agency vs in-house decision turns on three things: how many platforms you run, how much fresh creative you need and who will answer the leads. Be Leaded fits teams that want Meta, LinkedIn and X ads run outside the company with the lead follow up included.

This page is for B2B marketing leaders who need paid social to produce sales conversations and are deciding whether to hire for it or buy it.

The decision in brief

Build in house when paid social is a core, steady channel for you, when one or two platforms carry most of the budget and when you can fund the roles that write, design, buy media and report. Hire an agency when you need several platforms at once, more creative than one person can make and a working program sooner than a hiring plan allows.

Many B2B companies end up between the two. They keep positioning, budget and the sales relationship inside and hand campaign work to an outside partner. That split works when each side knows exactly what it owns.

The rest of this guide breaks the choice into the jobs that make up a paid social program, so you can decide job by job instead of all at once.

What B2B paid social covers on Meta, LinkedIn and X

Paid social for a B2B company is rarely one channel. Each platform does a different job in the buying journey and asks for different skills.

  • LinkedIn: targeting by job title, company and industry, which makes it the default for reaching named roles and target accounts
  • Meta (Facebook and Instagram): broad reach and retargeting, useful for staying in front of people who already visited your site or engaged with your content
  • X: conversation driven placements that suit launches, events and categories where buyers follow industry voices

A team that runs all three needs to understand three ad managers, three sets of creative formats and three ways of reporting. That breadth is often the first thing that pushes companies toward outside help.

The work behind a paid social program

Before you compare models, list the work. A B2B paid social program usually includes these jobs.

  • Strategy: which audiences, which offers and which platform gets which share of the budget
  • Creative: copy, static images, short video and carousel assets, refreshed often enough that audiences do not tune out
  • Media buying: campaign structure, bids, budgets, exclusions and daily checks
  • Landing pages and forms: where the click goes and what the form asks
  • Tracking: pixels, conversion events and the link from a form fill to a CRM record
  • Lead follow up: the first email or text after a form fill and the route to a sales conversation
  • Reporting: numbers that sales leadership trusts, tied to pipeline rather than clicks

Each job can sit inside or outside the company. The right answer for creative may be different from the right answer for reporting.

What an in-house setup looks like

An internal paid social function usually starts with one paid media manager and grows from there. That person often ends up covering strategy, buying and reporting while borrowing design time from the brand team.

The strengths are real. An internal manager sits next to sales, hears objections first hand and can change an offer the same afternoon. Knowledge of what worked stays with the company. Ad accounts, audiences and history are never in question.

The limits are also real. One person rarely has deep skill in three platforms plus creative plus analytics. Holidays and departures leave gaps. Creative output tends to fall behind because design time is shared with other priorities.

What an outside agency takes on

An agency brings a working process and a group of people who already know the platforms. You buy capacity and pattern recognition from other accounts rather than building it yourself.

The strengths: several platforms covered at once, creative produced as part of the service and a program that does not stop when one person is away. Good agencies also bring a test plan rather than waiting for you to set one.

The limits: an agency starts without your product context, needs clear briefs and can drift toward platform metrics if nobody asks for pipeline numbers. You also have to manage the relationship, which takes time on your side.

Signals that favor building inside

  • Paid social is a permanent, central channel for you and you expect to grow it for years
  • Most of the budget sits on one platform, usually LinkedIn, so depth matters more than breadth
  • You already have design and video capacity that can be dedicated to ads
  • Your product changes often and campaigns need daily input from product or sales
  • You can fund a salary plus tools plus creative without squeezing the media budget

If three or more of these hold, an internal hire is worth serious thought. Start with the role that covers the biggest gap, not with a full department.

Signals that favor an agency

  • You need to run Meta, LinkedIn and X together and nobody inside has run all three
  • Creative is the bottleneck and ads have not changed in a long time
  • Leads arrive but nobody follows up quickly or consistently
  • You want to test the channel before committing to permanent headcount
  • A previous hire left and took the account knowledge with them

Agencies are strongest where breadth and output matter. They are weakest when the brief is vague, so prepare your positioning, target accounts and sales process before you sign.

The split model: decisions inside, execution outside

A split keeps the decisions that depend on company knowledge inside and hands repeatable execution to a partner. A common division looks like this.

  • Inside: positioning, offers, budget across channels, approval of creative, the CRM and the relationship with sales
  • Outside: campaign builds, audience tests, creative production, bid and budget management and weekly reporting

Write the division down. Splits fail when both sides assume the other is setting direction. Name who decides which platform gets the next dollar and who decides when an ad is retired.

LinkedIn: where targeting depth decides the model

LinkedIn is usually the anchor of B2B paid social because you can reach people by role, seniority, company and industry. It is also the platform where small mistakes in targeting or bidding waste the most budget.

If LinkedIn carries most of your spend, depth matters. An internal hire who knows Campaign Manager well can do excellent work. An agency should show how it structures audiences, how it handles matched lists from your CRM and how it judges lead quality beyond form fills.

Either way, decide early how LinkedIn lead forms reach your CRM and who answers them. A form fill that waits days for a reply is budget spent twice.

Meta: reach and retargeting for B2B

Meta is not the first platform most B2B marketers think of, yet decision makers scroll Facebook and Instagram like everyone else. B2B teams use Meta to retarget site visitors, to reach lookalikes built from customer lists and to promote content to people already in their audience.

Meta rewards creative volume. Ads wear out faster than on LinkedIn, so the question is less about targeting skill and more about who will keep producing new angles. That often tips Meta work toward an agency or a split.

X: a smaller line item with its own habits

X tends to be a smaller share of B2B budgets. It suits companies whose buyers follow industry commentary there and it works well around launches and events.

Because it is a smaller line, few companies hire for it alone. If X matters to you, ask an agency how it plans X campaigns separately from Meta and LinkedIn instead of copying the same assets across.

Creative volume is usually the deciding factor

Ask most B2B teams why paid social stalled and the answer comes back to creative. The same three ads ran for too long, audiences stopped responding and nobody had time to make new ones.

An internal team can solve this with a dedicated designer and a simple production rhythm. An agency usually solves it by making creative part of the monthly scope. In a split, the agency produces and you approve, which keeps brand control inside.

Whatever you choose, plan creative as a steady stream rather than a launch event. Write the refresh cadence into the scope.

Measurement and the handoff to sales

Paid social only matters to a B2B business if it creates sales conversations. That makes tracking and follow up as important as the ads themselves.

  • Connect platform conversions to CRM records so each lead has a source
  • Agree with sales what counts as a qualified lead before launch
  • Set a rule for how fast a new lead gets a reply and who sends it
  • Report on meetings and pipeline next to cost per lead

This is where many agencies stop and many internal teams run out of time. Be Leaded includes the follow up by email or text, which is why we describe the work as ads with the follow up built in.

Who should own accounts, pixels and audiences

Whatever model you pick, your company should own the ad accounts, the business manager, the pixels, the matched audiences and the creative files. An agency should work inside your accounts with access you can revoke.

Check this before you sign. If an agency asks to run ads from its own accounts, ask what happens to your history and audiences if you part ways. With an internal hire, make sure access sits with the company and not only with one person's login.

Comparing costs without guessing

We do not publish cost estimates for either model because salaries, agency fees and media budgets vary too much by market. Instead, compare the full list of components.

  • In-house: salary and benefits, recruiting, tools for design and analytics, creative production you buy in and management time
  • Agency: the management fee, any creative or landing page fees, tools billed to you and your time for briefs and reviews
  • Both: the media budget itself, which is paid to the platforms either way

How company stage changes the answer

Early stage, founder led marketing

When the founder still writes most of the marketing, a full internal paid social role is hard to justify and harder to manage. An outside partner can run the channel while the founder keeps control of the message. The founder's job becomes approving offers and talking to the leads that come in.

First marketing hires in place

Once a small marketing team exists, the split model becomes practical. An internal marketer owns positioning and the CRM, while an agency handles platform work and creative. This is often where paid social first starts producing a steady flow of sales conversations.

Established demand function

Larger B2B marketing teams with steady budgets and clear targets often bring core platforms inside and keep an agency for creative capacity, new platforms or specific campaigns. The question shifts from whether to use outside help to which parts it should cover.

Reaching a buying group, not one buyer

B2B purchases usually involve several people: the person with the problem, a manager who signs off, finance and sometimes IT or procurement. Paid social is one of the few channels that can reach all of them with different messages at the same time.

That has a practical effect on the model you choose. Messaging for each role multiplies the creative you need and the audiences you manage. An internal team should plan for that workload. An agency should explain how it builds audiences by role and how it rotates messages for each one.

Whoever runs the program, connect it to the accounts your sales team already works. Ads that warm up the same companies your reps are calling make both efforts easier.

What a good brief looks like for either model

A new hire and a new agency both start from the same place: what you tell them. A short written brief saves a lot of rework.

  • The roles and company types you sell to, with examples of good customers
  • The problem you solve, in the words customers use
  • Offers you can make in an ad: a guide, a demo, an assessment or an event
  • What sales needs to know about each lead and how fast they will follow up
  • Brand rules, approval steps and who signs off on creative
  • The measures you will use to judge the program

Update the brief as you learn. It becomes the shared memory of the program, which matters most when people change on either side.

Reporting that sales leadership will trust

Platform dashboards report impressions, clicks and form fills. Those numbers help the person running campaigns but rarely persuade a sales leader. Agree on a short report that both sides read the same way.

  • Leads by platform and campaign, with the share that sales accepted
  • Meetings booked from paid social leads
  • Pipeline created from contacts who engaged with ads
  • Spend by platform next to those outcomes
  • What changed this period and what will be tested next

Ask for this report from an agency in the first conversation. With an internal hire, build it together in the first weeks so it becomes the habit rather than an afterthought.

Lead forms, consent and the first reply

Native lead forms on LinkedIn and Meta make it easy for buyers to raise a hand. They also make it easy to collect leads you cannot contact the way you planned. Decide before launch what the form says about follow up by email, phone or text and keep that wording visible.

Then decide who sends the first reply and how. A form fill is a moment of interest that fades quickly. Whether the reply comes from a rep, a marketing automation flow or a partner, it should arrive while the buyer still remembers the ad.

This step is easy to leave out of an agency scope and easy to forget when an internal manager is busy. Name the owner in writing.

Paid social next to outbound and email

Paid social works harder when it runs alongside other ways of reaching the same accounts. Ads build familiarity, so a cold email or a call from a rep lands with someone who has seen the name before. Retargeting can follow up with people who visited after an email.

If you plan to combine channels, the model question extends beyond ads. Someone has to coordinate lists, timing and messages across them. Some companies keep that coordination inside; others hand ads and outreach to the same partner so the pieces fit together.

Questions to put to any agency

  • Which platforms do you run for B2B companies today and how do you split budget between them?
  • How often do you refresh creative and who makes it?
  • Do you work inside our ad accounts and can we revoke access at any time?
  • How do leads reach our CRM and who follows up first?
  • Which numbers do you report and how do they connect to pipeline?
  • What do you need from us each month to do good work?

Questions to answer before you hire

  • Which single gap would this person close first: buying, creative or reporting?
  • Who will this person learn from if nobody else inside runs paid social?
  • Where will creative come from once the first assets wear out?
  • What happens to the program while you recruit and while the new hire ramps up?
  • How will you know after a fair period whether the hire is working?

If the answers are vague, a split or an agency may cover the gap while you build the internal role over time.

Testing a partner before you commit

You can lower the risk of either choice with a defined first period. Agree on scope, platforms, creative volume and the measures you will judge it by. Keep the scope narrow enough that results are readable.

With an agency, look for terms that let you stop if the work is not right for you. With a hire, set clear expectations for the first quarter and check in on them openly.

Moving from one model to the other

Companies move in both directions. Some start with an agency and build inside once the channel proves itself for them. Others lose an internal manager and bring in a partner to keep the program running.

  • Document campaign structure, audiences, exclusions and naming before any handover
  • Export creative files and performance history
  • Keep the old and new owners overlapping for a short period
  • Change access only after the new owner confirms everything works

Mistakes that make either model fail

  • Judging on cost per lead alone: cheap leads that sales never speaks to cost more in the end
  • No owner for follow up: leads wait in a spreadsheet while the buyer moves on
  • One creative set for every platform: each platform rewards its own formats
  • Unclear ownership in a split: both sides wait for the other to decide
  • Accounts in someone else's name: history and audiences are lost at the worst moment

Most of these are process problems rather than talent problems. Fix them on paper first and either model has a fair chance.

One last test helps. Picture the program a year from now and ask who will know why each campaign exists, which audiences worked and what the next test is. If the honest answer is nobody, the model you pick matters less than putting that knowledge somewhere the company can keep it. A shared brief, a simple test log and named owners do more for long term results than any single hire or contract.

Frequently asked questions

Is it better to hire a B2B paid social agency or build in-house?

It depends on platform breadth, creative needs and who answers leads. In-house suits one steady core platform with dedicated design capacity. An agency suits several platforms at once and a need for more creative. Many B2B teams use a split.

Which tasks does an outside paid social partner handle for a B2B company?

Typically it plans and runs campaigns on platforms such as Meta, LinkedIn and X, produces creative, manages budgets and bids, sets up tracking and reports results. Some also build landing pages and handle lead follow up.

Which platforms matter most for B2B paid social?

LinkedIn is usually the anchor because of job title and company targeting. Meta adds reach and retargeting. X suits launches, events and categories where buyers follow industry voices.

What is a hybrid or split paid social model?

Your company keeps positioning, budget, creative approval and the sales relationship. An outside partner handles campaign builds, testing, creative production and reporting. It works best with ownership written down.

Who should own the ad accounts if I hire an agency?

Your company should own the ad accounts, pixels, audiences and creative files. The agency should work inside them with access you can remove.

How long should I give an agency before judging it?

Agree on a defined first period and the measures up front, such as accepted leads, meetings and pipeline. Judge against those rather than early click metrics.

Can an in-house marketer and an agency work together?

Yes. The internal marketer keeps positioning, budget and the sales relationship. The agency runs campaign builds, creative and testing. Write down who decides what.

Is paid social worth it for B2B companies?

It can be when targeting, creative and follow up are all in place. LinkedIn reaches named roles, Meta adds reach and retargeting and X suits launches and events. Leads that nobody answers are the most common reason it disappoints.

How is Be Leaded paid social priced?

What should I prepare before talking to an agency?

Your positioning, target roles and accounts, current ad account access, how leads reach sales today and what counts as a qualified lead for your team.

Want a second opinion before you hire?

Bring your current ad accounts and the way leads reach sales today. We will say plainly which parts make sense to keep inside and which parts we would take on. Month to month, no long-term contract.
