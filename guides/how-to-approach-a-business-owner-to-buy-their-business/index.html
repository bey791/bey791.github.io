Who this playbook is for

This guide is for a buyer making the first approach to an owner whose company is not for sale. That might be a searcher, a business owner who wants to buy a competitor or a supplier, a family investment office or a first time buyer who has saved up to buy and run one company.

It starts after you have a name. Finding owners who might sell, reading timing signals and building the list are covered in our article on how to find business owners who want to sell. This page is about what you say once you have an owner in front of you, how you say it and what happens next.

It is not for brokers marketing a listing to buyers. If that is your job, our guides on how business brokers find buyers and business broker marketing are a better place to start.

Three things to settle before the first message

Owners can tell within a few lines whether a buyer has done any thinking. Before you write, make sure you can say three things plainly.

  • Who you are. Your name, what you do now and why you want to own a business like this one. One or two sentences.
  • Why this company. A specific reason that comes from the business itself: its market, its customers, its reputation or how it would fit something you already own.
  • What you are asking for. A private conversation. Not a sale, not an offer and not their accounts.

If you cannot write those three things for a company, you are not ready to contact its owner. Go back to your list and pick a company you can describe properly.

Reach the right person

Write to the owner, not to a general inbox or the office manager. In a small company the owner is usually the founder or a family member, but not always. Check public company records, the company website and the owner's LinkedIn profile to confirm who holds the shares.

If two partners own the business, write to both, separately, with the same message. Sending only to one can cause friction between them. If a parent company sits above the business, find the people who own that company.

Avoid contacting staff, customers or suppliers about a possible sale. Word travels fast in small markets and an owner who hears about your interest second hand is unlikely to trust you.

Choosing letter, email, LinkedIn or phone

Each channel has a different feel. Pick the one that suits the owner, not the one that is easiest for you.

  • A letter. A signed letter on paper stands out because so few arrive. It suits older owners and traditional trades. It is slow to send and slow to follow up.
  • Email. Quick, easy to reply to and easy to follow up. It also lands next to a lot of sales mail, so the subject line and first sentence need to sound like a person.
  • LinkedIn. Worth using if the owner posts or replies there. A short note with a clear reason works better than a long pitch in a connection request.
  • Phone. Direct and personal. It also catches owners at a busy moment, so keep it short and use it to follow up a letter or email rather than as a cold first touch.

Many buyers combine channels: a letter or email first, a LinkedIn note or a short call a couple of weeks later and a final note after that.

What to say in the first message

A good first message is short enough to read in under a minute. It has four parts in this order.

  • A greeting by name and one sentence on who you are.
  • One or two sentences on why you are writing to this owner about this company, with a detail only someone who looked at the business would know.
  • A clear, small ask: a private, no obligation conversation at a time that suits them.
  • Your direct phone number and email, so they can reply to you and not to a team.

Leave out price, valuation multiples, deal structure and deadlines. Leave out jargon such as "platform" or "synergies." Write the way you would talk to them across a table.

A first letter you can adapt

Use this as a starting point and rewrite it in your own words. The details in brackets are where the specific reason goes.

"Dear [owner's first name], my name is [your name]. I [one line on what you do now] and I am looking to buy and run one [type of] business in [region] for the long term. I came across [company] because [specific, accurate reason]. Selling may not be on your mind at all, which is fine. If it is ever worth talking about, I would like to introduce myself in a private conversation. There is no obligation and anything you share stays between us. You can reach me directly at [phone] or [email]. Thank you for reading this. [Your name]"

Sign it by hand if you send it on paper. Keep it to one page.

A short first email you can adapt

Email needs to be even shorter than a letter. Aim for five or six sentences.

Subject: "A question about [company]"

"Hi [first name], I am [your name] and I am looking to buy one [type of] business in [region] and run it myself. [Company] stood out because [specific reason]. Would you be open to a short private call sometime, with no obligation? If the timing is wrong, no reply is needed. [Your name], [phone]"

Send it from your own address, not a shared mailbox. A real name and a real number make the message easier to trust.

What to say on a first call

If you call, assume the owner is busy. Introduce yourself, mention the note you sent and ask whether they have two minutes. If not, ask when would be better and end the call.

If they do have time, keep it light. Ask about the business and what they enjoy about running it. Ask what they hope happens to it in the long run. Listen more than you talk. Do not ask for numbers and do not push for a meeting in the first minute.

End by asking for one small next step, usually a longer conversation in person or by video at a time they choose. Then send a short note the same day confirming what you agreed.

Following up without pressure

Expect no answer to your first message from most owners. A lack of reply tells you very little. The note may be sitting in a pile, the owner may be thinking it over or they may want proof that you will write again.

A calm sequence works better than a fast one. Send the first note. Two or three weeks later, follow up by a different channel to make sure it arrived. A few weeks after that, send a short final note saying you will not keep writing but the door stays open. Then stop.

Chasing an owner every week reads as impatience. It can also hurt your reputation with other owners in the same trade or town, who often know each other.

Answering objections about price and timing

Owners who do reply often start with a reason not to talk. Treat each one as a question, not a rejection.

  • "I am not selling." Thank them, say you understand and ask whether you may stay in touch once a year. Some owners change their minds after a health scare or a family conversation.
  • "What would you pay?" Say honestly that you do not know enough to name a figure yet and would not want to guess. Offer to talk about how you would value it once you understand the business.
  • "Not now, maybe in a few years." Ask what would need to happen first. Note the answer and agree when to check in.
  • "I have had lots of these letters." Acknowledge it. Explain what makes you different: you want to own and run this one company, not resell it and you can say why.

Never argue. An owner who feels pushed will remember it.

Answering objections about staff, customers and trust

  • "What happens to my people?" Say what you actually plan. If you intend to keep the team, say so plainly. If you are not sure yet, say that too.
  • "How do I know you can fund it?" Explain where the money would come from in general terms and say you will provide proof at the right stage.
  • "Is this a scam or a data grab?" Give them ways to check you out: your full name, a number that you answer yourself, an online profile that tells the same story as your letter and no requests for documents.
  • "Will my customers find out?" Explain how you keep conversations private and that nothing goes to anyone else without their agreement.

These questions are often more important to the owner than price. Taking them seriously is how you earn a second conversation.

Keeping the conversation confidential

A common fear for owners is that staff, customers or competitors will hear that they are talking to a buyer. You cannot control every rumor, but you can act carefully from the first message.

  • Keep every message addressed to the owner alone until the owner widens the circle.
  • Do not mention the approach to anyone who works with or for the company.
  • Use the owner's personal email or a letter marked private and confidential if you can.
  • Do not leave detailed voicemails at the business line.
  • Offer to sign a confidentiality agreement before any figures are shared.

Say clearly in your first meeting how you will handle what they tell you. That alone reassures many owners.

Moving from first reply to first meeting

Once an owner agrees to talk, keep the first meeting small and relaxed. Meet at a time and place they choose. Bring no lawyers or advisers. The goal is to get to know each other, not to start a deal.

Ask open questions about how they started, what they are proud of and what worries them about the future. Share your own background honestly. At the end, ask whether they would like to meet again and what they would want to know before then.

When documents and numbers come in

Financial information usually comes after trust, not before it. Once an owner is open to talking seriously, offer a simple confidentiality agreement. Then ask for a small set of figures, such as a few years of revenue and profit, rather than everything at once.

Keep the pace in the owner's hands. If you rush to an offer, you risk losing the owner. If you never move forward, they may assume you are not serious. A short written summary after each meeting helps both sides see where things stand.

If you already know the owner

Sometimes the owner is a customer, a supplier, a former colleague or someone you meet at trade events. That makes the first contact easier and riskier at the same time. Easier because they already know who you are. Riskier because a clumsy approach can damage a relationship you rely on.

Ask in private, never at a busy event or in front of their staff. Frame it as a question about the future, not an offer: "Have you ever thought about what happens to the business when you step back? If you ever want to talk about it, I would be interested." Then let them set the pace. If they change the subject, drop it and carry on as before.

Make it clear that the existing relationship does not depend on their answer. Owners who feel that a no would cost them a customer or a supplier will often say no just to stay safe.

If you run a company in the same trade

Owners of competing or neighboring businesses often look at buying small companies nearby. They are also the buyers an owner is most nervous about, because a competitor who learns the numbers and walks away has gained an edge.

Be direct about who you are from the start. Hiding that you are a competitor and revealing it later destroys trust. Explain why the two businesses would fit together and what would stay the same for the owner's team and customers.

Offer more protection than usual on confidentiality. Suggest that early numbers be shared at a summary level or through an adviser and keep the circle on your side small. Respecting that worry is often what gets a competitor a first meeting at all.

Taking a no well

A polite no is still useful. Thank the owner for replying, say you respect the decision and ask, lightly, whether they would mind if you checked in once in a year or two. Many will say yes. A few will tell you what would change their mind.

Write down exactly what they said and the date. Then honor it. If they asked you not to contact them again, do not. If they said maybe later, put a reminder in your calendar and come back with a fresh note that mentions the last exchange.

Check each message before it goes out

  • Is the owner's name spelled correctly and is the company name right?
  • Does the note give one specific, accurate reason for writing to this company?
  • Is the ask a conversation, with no price, deadline or request for documents?
  • Would you be comfortable if the owner forwarded it to their family or their staff?
  • Can the owner reply to you directly by phone or email?
  • Is it short enough to read in under a minute?

A quick check like this catches most of the mistakes that cost a buyer a reply.

Mistakes that end conversations early

  • Leading with a price or a multiple in the first message.
  • Sending a mail merge with the wrong company name or a generic compliment.
  • Asking for financial statements before a first conversation.
  • Contacting employees or customers to ask about the owner.
  • Following up every few days.
  • Hiding who the buyer is or why you are writing.
  • Making commitments about staff or terms that you cannot keep.

Each of these is easy to avoid with a little care and each one can close a door for good.

When the owner already has a broker or adviser

Sometimes an owner replies that they are already working with a broker or an M&A adviser. Respect that. Ask whether you may send your details to the adviser and be considered as a buyer. Do not try to go around the adviser.

If you want to understand how a broker runs a sale and what they look for in a buyer, the broker guides linked above explain how brokers market a listing and find buyers. Knowing that process helps you present yourself as a serious buyer when an adviser is involved.

Keeping records and coming back later

Keep a simple record for every owner you contact: the date of each message, the channel, the reply and the next step. Mark each owner as interested, not now, silent or no.

Respect a clear no and do not contact that owner again unless they invite it. Review the not now and silent owners once a year or so. Some conversations start with a second approach years after the first, when the owner's situation has changed.

Frequently asked questions

How do you approach a business owner about buying their business?

Write to the owner personally with a short note that says who you are, why this company and that you would value a private conversation. Leave out price and pressure, follow up a few times at a calm pace and keep a record of every reply.

What should I say in a first letter to an owner?

Introduce yourself in a sentence, give a specific reason for writing about this company, ask for a private conversation with no obligation and give your direct phone number and email. Keep it to one page.

Should I mention price in the first message?

No. At this stage you cannot know what the company is worth. A figure named too early either sets a ceiling you regret or offends the owner. Talk about value once you understand the business.

Is email or a letter better for a first approach?

Both work. A signed letter stands out and suits traditional owners. Email is faster and easier to answer. Many buyers send one first and follow up with the other.

How many times should I follow up?

Usually two or three times over a couple of months, using a different channel each time, then a final note saying the door stays open. Chasing every week tends to put owners off.

How do I keep the approach confidential?

Write only to the owner, avoid business voicemails and staff, use personal email or a private letter and offer a confidentiality agreement before any figures are shared.
