What a customer acquisition agency does for a B2B company

A customer acquisition agency is an outside team paid to bring you new buyers. That sounds broad because it is. Some firms send cold email and make calls. Some run ads on social platforms. Some write content and work on search so buyers find you on their own. What they share is the brief: more new customers at a cost per customer your business can carry.

That last part is what separates acquisition work from general marketing. A brand campaign can succeed without a single new account. An acquisition program cannot. When you hire for acquisition you are asking a firm to put its work next to a sales number, so the useful questions are about which buyers it will reach, how a stranger becomes a meeting and how you will know which part of the spend produced which customer.

For a B2B company the path is usually longer than for a shop selling to consumers. Several people at the buying company weigh in, a sales call is often needed and the deal may take weeks or months. A good acquisition firm plans for that. It knows that its job ends at a qualified conversation with your sales team or at a signed deal if it also runs the follow up. It reports on that point rather than on clicks.

Three ways firms win customers: outbound, paid social and inbound

Almost every B2B acquisition firm leans on one of three motions. Knowing which one a firm leads with tells you more about the proposal than any capabilities slide.

  • Outbound. The firm picks the companies and people you want, then contacts them directly by email, phone, LinkedIn or post. You choose the list. Results depend on list quality, the message and how fast replies are handled.
  • Paid social. The firm buys attention on Meta, LinkedIn, X and similar networks, shows ads to an audience it defines and sends interested people to a form or page. You pay the platforms for reach and the firm for running it.
  • Inbound. The firm builds content, search visibility and the systems behind your website so buyers who are already looking find you and ask for a call. It is slower to start and keeps working after the effort stops.

Many firms sell more than one motion. Still, most have a center of gravity: the work their senior people know best and the service most of their clients buy. The groups below follow that center of gravity, based on how each firm describes itself on its own site.

When outbound should be your first motion

Outbound tends to be the right first motion when you can name your buyers. If you could write down the companies you want as customers and the job titles that sign, a firm can reach them this month without waiting for them to search or scroll.

It also suits deals that are worth enough to justify a direct approach. Writing to a few hundred well chosen people a week only pays off when one new account covers many weeks of effort. For small, quick purchases the cost of each conversation is usually too high.

Outbound is a poor first motion when your market does not yet know the problem you solve. Cold messages work best when the reader already feels the pain and simply did not know you existed. If you need to teach the problem first, paid social or content will usually carry that message more cheaply.

Look at the size of your reachable market as well. If only a few thousand companies could ever buy from you, a firm can work through that list with care and come back to it each quarter with a new angle. If the market is far larger, outbound still works, but list choice becomes the main skill and you should ask each firm how it decides who to contact first.

Finally, check your own follow up. Outbound produces replies at odd hours from busy people. If nobody on your side answers within the day, a share of those replies will cool before your sales team ever sees them. A firm that books the meeting for you removes much of that risk.

When paid social should be your first motion

Paid social is a strong first motion when your buyers are easy to describe but hard to list. If you know the job, the industry and the company size but could not name every account, social platforms can find those people for you at a scale outbound cannot.

It also helps when your offer needs to be seen before it is bought. A short video, a clear before and after or a simple demo can do in a few seconds what a cold email struggles to do in a paragraph. Retargeting then keeps you in front of people who visited your site and left.

The weak spot is the step after the click. A form fill from a social ad is a person who was mildly interested for a moment. If nobody calls or writes back quickly, most of those leads go nowhere and the ads get blamed. Before you hire a paid social firm, decide who replies to each lead and how fast. If nobody can, hire a firm that does that part too.

Paid social is a poor first motion when your budget is too small to test more than one audience. Platforms need enough spend to learn who responds. A plan that splits a small budget across three platforms usually teaches you very little.

When inbound should be your first motion

Inbound is the right first motion when buyers already search for what you sell. If people type the name of your category into a search box each month, being present for those searches is often the most efficient acquisition you will find.

It also suits companies with patience and a point of view. Content that earns attention takes months to build and rank. The payoff is that good pages keep bringing in visitors long after they are written, which no ad or email sequence does.

Inbound is a weak first motion when you need new customers this quarter, when your category is so new that nobody searches for it yet or when your best buyers are senior people who rarely fill in a form after reading a blog post. In those cases it works better as a second motion that supports outbound or paid social.

Check one thing before you hire an inbound firm: who turns a content visit into a sales conversation. A page that brings in readers but never asks them to talk is a library, not an acquisition program.

Picking a motion by deal size and sales cycle

Deal size is the quickest filter. The more one new customer is worth, the more you can spend to reach each buyer and the more a direct, personal approach makes sense.

  • Large deals, long cycles, a known list of accounts. Outbound usually leads. Paid social on LinkedIn can warm the same accounts before and after the outreach. Inbound content helps the buyer's colleagues check you out.
  • Mid sized deals, a clear job title but no fixed list. Paid social and outbound often work together. Ads reach the wider pool while outreach goes to the companies that fit best.
  • Small deals, short cycles, a self serve product. Paid social and inbound tend to carry the load, because the cost of a one to one conversation is too high for the value of each sale.

Sales cycle length matters for a second reason: it sets how long you need to wait before judging a firm. A firm that books meetings for a deal that closes in six months cannot show you closed revenue in month two. Agree up front which earlier signal you will use, such as qualified meetings held. Agree when you expect to see it too.

Picking a motion by how aware your buyers already are

The second filter is how much your buyers already know. Some markets are full of people who feel the problem and are comparing options. Others barely know the problem exists.

When buyers are actively looking, inbound and search content catch them at the moment of interest. Outbound can reach the ones who have not found you yet. When buyers feel the pain but have not started looking, outbound with a sharp message and paid social with a clear offer can start the conversation. When buyers do not know the problem exists, paid social and content that explain the problem usually need to come first, because a cold email asking for a meeting about an unknown problem rarely lands.

Your own data can settle the question faster than any pitch. Look at where your current customers first heard of you. If most came through introductions and direct conversations, outbound copies a pattern that already works. If many found you through a search or an article, inbound has room to grow. If a share came from an ad or a social post, paid social deserves a proper test with a real budget.

A useful test is to read your last ten sales calls. If most prospects arrived already describing the problem in their own words, you can lead with direct outreach. If your team spent the first call explaining why the problem matters, you need a motion that teaches before it asks.

Running two motions without splitting the budget too thin

Many B2B companies end up running two motions, often outbound plus paid social or paid social plus inbound. That can work well. The risk is spreading a modest budget so thin that neither motion gets enough volume to learn from.

A practical rule is to fund one motion properly first, run it until you understand what a qualified meeting costs, then add the second. When you do add it, connect the two. Retarget people who opened your emails with social ads. Write to the companies whose staff clicked your ads. Use the questions buyers ask on calls as topics for content.

If you hire two firms, make one of them responsible for the shared report. You can also keep it yourself. Two firms each reporting their own numbers will each take credit for the same customer. One firm that runs both motions avoids that argument, which is part of why some companies prefer it.

What customer acquisition costs: the fee, ad spend, tools and your time

The fee on the proposal is only part of what a program costs. Before you compare firms, list every line so you are comparing like with like.

  • The firm's fee. Firms charge flat each month, as a share of ad spend, per meeting or with some mix of those. Ask what happens to the fee when volume goes up or down.
  • Media or ad spend. For paid social this goes to the platforms. Check whether the firm's fee is a share of it, because then the fee rises as you scale.
  • Data and tools. Contact data, sending tools, enrichment and tracking. Some firms include these in the fee. Others pass them on.
  • Content and creative. Ads, videos, landing pages and articles. Ask how many are included and who owns them.
  • Your team's time. Briefing, approvals, sales calls and follow up. A cheap program that needs ten hours a week from your head of sales is not cheap.

Working out what a new customer can cost you

Before any firm quotes, work out the most a single new customer is worth paying for. You need three inputs from your own records: what an average new customer is worth to you over the first year or two, what share of qualified sales calls turn into customers and how many calls your team can handle each month.

Multiply backwards from there. If you know what a customer is worth and what share of calls close, you know the most you can pay for a qualified call and still make money. Compare that ceiling with each firm's full monthly cost divided by the number of qualified calls you would need. If the numbers only work in the most hopeful case, the motion or the firm is wrong for now.

This sum matters more than the size of the fee. A firm with a higher fee that brings better fit buyers can cost less per customer than a cheap firm whose leads never close. Share your ceiling with the firms you talk to. Good ones will tell you plainly whether they can work inside it.

What the first 90 days should look like

Most acquisition programs follow the same arc in their first three months, whichever motion they use. Knowing the arc helps you judge whether a firm is on track.

  • Weeks one to four: setup. Agree the buyer, build the list or the audiences, set up tracking, write the first messages or ads and connect everything to your CRM. Little should go out before the tracking works.
  • Weeks five to eight: first tests. Messages and ads go live in small batches. The firm should report which audiences and angles get replies or clicks and should already be cutting the weak ones.
  • Weeks nine to twelve: first verdict. You should see qualified conversations from the motion, even if few have closed yet. The firm should explain what it will change next and why.

Inbound runs on a slower clock than this. Content takes longer to rank, so the verdict at twelve weeks is about output and early signs rather than meetings. Agree that difference before you start so nobody is surprised.

Questions to ask before you sign

Send the same questions to every firm and ask for written answers. Comparing written answers side by side shows the differences that a polished pitch hides.

  • Which motion would you lead with for us and why not the other two?
  • Who exactly will work on our account each week and what else are they working on?
  • What will you need from us in the first month and how much of our time will that take?
  • Which number will you report weekly and where does it come from?
  • What happens to a reply or a lead in the first hour after it arrives?
  • What does the full monthly cost come to, with ad spend, data and tools included?
  • What do we keep if we stop working together?

Firms that answer these clearly and in writing are usually easier to work with later. Firms that push every answer to a call may simply be busy, but it is worth noticing.

Contracts, account ownership and data

Read the contract terms as closely as the plan. Three things matter most: how long you are committed, what you own and what happens at the end.

On length, a short first phase is reasonable while the firm learns your market. A long minimum term before you have seen any results shifts the risk onto you. Ask whether the firm offers month to month terms after a setup period. Get any notice period in writing.

On ownership, insist that your ad accounts, sending domains, lists, content and CRM data sit in accounts your company controls. The firm should have access you can remove. If a firm builds everything inside its own accounts, leaving it means starting again.

On exit, ask what the handover includes: exported lists, audiences, ads, copy, content files and a written summary of what worked. A firm confident in its work will usually agree to that without fuss.

Red flags in an acquisition pitch

A few warning signs come up again and again when B2B companies hire acquisition firms. None of them proves a firm is wrong for you, but each deserves a direct question.

  • Exact results stated as a certainty before the firm has seen your offer, your market or your past data.
  • Every motion recommended at once, from day one, with an even budget split.
  • Reports built on opens, clicks or impressions with no line for qualified conversations.
  • No clear answer on who will do the work each week.
  • Your accounts, lists or content kept inside the firm's own systems.
  • A long minimum term with no review point.

If a firm shows two or more of these, ask it to put its plan and its terms in writing and compare the result with the other firms on your list.

When an agency is the wrong answer

Sometimes no acquisition firm will help. If your product does not yet solve a clear problem for a clear buyer, more outreach or more ads will only bring more polite rejections. Spend the time talking to customers first.

An outside firm is also a poor fit when nobody on your side can take the calls it books. Acquisition creates demand for your sales team's time. If that time is not there, the firm's work is wasted and you will pay for meetings that never happen.

Finally, if your budget can only cover a few weeks, hold off. Every motion needs a setup period and a few rounds of testing. A program cut short in month two tells you almost nothing about whether the motion could have worked.

Frequently asked questions

What is a customer acquisition agency?

It is an outside firm paid to bring a company new customers. B2B firms usually lead with one of three motions: outbound outreach by email, phone and LinkedIn, paid social ads on social platforms like Meta, LinkedIn and X or inbound content and search work.

Which customer acquisition motion should a B2B company start with?

Start with outbound when you can name your target accounts and each deal is worth a direct approach. Start with paid social when you can describe the buyer but not list every account. Start with inbound when buyers already search for what you sell and you can wait for content to build.

How much do customer acquisition agencies charge?

It varies by firm and motion. Some publish starting fees or minimum ad budgets on their own sites while others scope the fee on a call. Compare the full monthly cost, with ad spend, data, tools and your own team's time included, against what one new customer is worth to you.

How long before a customer acquisition program shows results?

Expect a setup period of about a month, then a few weeks of tests. Outbound and paid social should produce qualified conversations within the first three months. Inbound runs on a slower clock because content takes time to rank.

Should I hire one firm or one per channel?

One firm that runs two motions avoids double counting and keeps one report. Separate firms can bring more depth in each channel but someone has to own the shared numbers. Fund one motion properly before adding a second.

What should I own when I hire an acquisition firm?

Your ad accounts, sending domains, contact lists, content and CRM data should sit in accounts your company controls. The firm should work with access you can remove. The handover at the end should include lists, ads, copy and a summary of what worked.
