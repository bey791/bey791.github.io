Short answer

There is no single best LinkedIn Ads agency for every B2B company. The right firm depends on whether you want LinkedIn only or LinkedIn inside a broader paid plan, whether you need creative produced for you and how closely the agency should connect ads to your CRM and sales process.

Below are ten firms that sell LinkedIn Ads management to B2B companies, each described from its own website. Use the profiles to build a shortlist of three, then put the same questions to each.

What a LinkedIn Ads agency actually runs

A LinkedIn Ads engagement normally covers the work inside Campaign Manager plus the pieces around it that decide whether ads turn into pipeline.

  • Account and campaign structure: objectives, campaign groups and naming that make reporting readable
  • Audiences: job titles, seniority, company lists, matched audiences from your CRM and retargeting pools
  • Creative and copy: single image, carousel, video, document and message formats
  • Bids and budgets: pacing across campaigns and shifting spend toward what works
  • Tracking: the Insight Tag, conversion events and the link from a lead form to your CRM
  • Reporting: results tied to meetings and pipeline, not only clicks and form fills

When you compare agencies, check which of these each one includes and which it expects you to handle.

LinkedIn ad formats to discuss with any agency

LinkedIn offers several formats and each suits a different job. An agency should explain which it would use for your offer and why.

  • Single image ads: the workhorse format for awareness and lead capture
  • Carousel ads: several cards in one ad, useful for features, steps or use cases
  • Video ads: short explanations and product views for colder audiences
  • Document ads: a guide or report people can preview in the feed, often gated by a lead form
  • Message and conversation ads: sponsored messages with buttons that guide the reader to an offer
  • Thought leader ads: promoted posts from people at your company rather than the company page
  • Text and spotlight ads: small placements on desktop for retargeting and reminders
  • Lead gen forms: forms that open inside LinkedIn with profile data filled in

No format is right for everyone. Ask each agency which two or three it would start with and what would make it change course.

Targeting questions that separate strong firms

Targeting is the reason B2B teams pay LinkedIn's premium. It is also where weak setups waste the most money. These questions reveal how an agency thinks.

  • How do you balance job title targeting against job function and seniority?
  • Will you use our CRM lists for matched audiences and exclusions?
  • How do you keep audiences large enough to deliver without becoming too broad?
  • How do you handle LinkedIn's audience expansion setting?
  • What retargeting pools would you build first from site visitors, video viewers and form openers?

Listen for specifics. A firm that answers with your market in mind is more useful than one that recites platform features.

Measuring LinkedIn Ads beyond cost per lead

Cost per lead is easy to report and easy to game. Lead gen forms can produce many contacts who never speak to sales. Agree on measures that reflect pipeline before the first campaign launches.

  • Share of leads that sales accepts as qualified
  • Meetings booked and held from LinkedIn leads
  • Pipeline and closed revenue from accounts reached by ads
  • Engagement from target accounts on your named list
  • Spend by campaign next to those outcomes

Several firms above describe CRM connected reporting on their sites. Ask to see a sample report and check whether it would answer the questions your sales leader asks.

Creative and how often it should change

B2B audiences on LinkedIn are narrow, so the same people see your ads repeatedly. Creative wears out faster than many teams expect. Ask who produces new ads, how many variations run at once and what signals trigger a refresh.

Some firms produce creative inside the agency. Others brief your designers or advise on best practices. Neither is wrong, but you need to know which you are buying so the work does not stall when the first assets tire.

How LinkedIn Ads agencies structure fees

We do not list agency prices because they change and depend on spend and scope. You will usually meet one of these structures.

  • Flat monthly fee: a set management fee, often in tiers tied to ad spend
  • Share of ad spend: a fee calculated as a portion of what you spend on LinkedIn
  • Project or audit fee: a one off review or setup before ongoing management
  • Bundled scope: LinkedIn priced together with other channels, creative or landing pages

Ask what the fee includes, what costs extra and what the notice terms are. Media spend is paid to LinkedIn separately in almost every model.

  • Who builds creative and how many new ads should we expect each month?
  • How do leads reach our CRM and how do you learn which ones became meetings?
  • What will you change first in our current account and why?

Warning signs during selection

  • Reporting that stops at clicks, impressions and cost per lead
  • Ads run from the agency's own account instead of yours
  • A plan built before anyone asks about your sales process
  • Vague answers about who makes creative
  • Results quoted with no way to check them

None of these rules a firm out on its own. Together they suggest the agency is optimizing for its own reporting rather than your pipeline.

When to keep LinkedIn Ads in house

An agency is not always the answer. If you already have someone who knows Campaign Manager well, a steady budget on LinkedIn and design capacity for new ads, keeping the work inside may serve you better. You keep context and control.

In that case, a consulting engagement or an audit from one of the firms above can still help. Several describe account reviews or training for teams that run their own ads.

What the first period with an agency should include

  • A review of the current account, tracking and audiences
  • Agreement on target roles, accounts and offers
  • A clear test plan with the first campaigns and formats
  • CRM connection checked end to end with a test lead
  • A reporting format agreed with marketing and sales

Avoid judging the program on early click metrics. Give it the period you agreed and judge it on the measures you set together.

Why lead follow up decides LinkedIn Ads results

A LinkedIn lead form is a moment of interest. If the first reply comes days later, the lead often no longer remembers the ad. Many teams blame the agency or the platform when the real gap sits between the form and sales.

Before you hire, decide who sends the first reply, how fast and through which channel. If no one can own that step reliably, choose a partner that includes it in the scope.

Why B2B teams pay for LinkedIn Ads at all

LinkedIn clicks often cost more than Google or Meta clicks, as KlientBoost's LinkedIn Ads page notes. Teams still pay for it because it is the one major ad platform where people describe their own job, company, seniority and industry. That lets you reach the finance director at a target account rather than anyone who searched a keyword or liked a related page.

The trade off is reach and cost. Audiences are small next to Meta or Google, clicks cost more and the people you want are busy. A good agency earns its fee by making that narrow, costly audience work harder: tighter targeting, offers worth the attention and creative that does not look like every other software ad in the feed.

If your buyers are easy to reach by search or your deal size is small, LinkedIn may not be the right first channel. Any agency on this list should be willing to say so. A firm that recommends LinkedIn for every situation is selling a channel, not solving your problem.

Campaign objectives and what each one buys you

Campaign Manager asks you to choose an objective before anything else and that choice shapes who sees your ads and how you pay. Agencies should explain their reasoning for each campaign.

  • Brand awareness: paid by impressions, used to warm a target audience that has never heard of you
  • Engagement and video views: builds retargeting pools of people who interacted with your content
  • Website visits: sends people to a page you control, where the Insight Tag can track them
  • Lead generation: opens a form inside LinkedIn with profile details filled in
  • Website conversions: optimizes delivery toward people likely to complete an action on your site

Many B2B programs use more than one objective at once: awareness or video to warm accounts, then lead generation or conversions for people who have engaged. Ask how the agency would split budget between those stages and what would make them shift it.

Lead gen forms or your own landing page

This choice comes up in almost every LinkedIn Ads program and it deserves a direct conversation with any agency you consider.

LinkedIn lead gen forms are convenient. The form opens without leaving the feed and fills in name, company and title from the profile. That convenience usually raises form completions. It can also lower intent, because submitting takes one tap and some people do it without much thought.

A landing page asks for more effort. People who complete it have read more about the offer and you control the message, the questions and the tracking. The cost is that fewer people finish.

Neither option is right in every case. A sensible agency tests both, adds qualifying questions to native forms when lead quality slips and judges each route on meetings held rather than raw lead count. Ask who builds and maintains landing pages, since some firms include them and others expect your team to do it.

Account based campaigns on LinkedIn

Most firms in this guide mention account based work. On LinkedIn that usually means uploading a list of target companies, layering role and seniority filters on top and running ads only to people at those accounts.

The approach works best when sales and marketing agree on the list. If the list is a guess, the ads reach the wrong companies with great precision. Before an agency builds anything, settle which accounts matter, who owns them on the sales side and what counts as engagement worth acting on.

Ask how the agency will report on account engagement. Useful reporting shows which target companies are seeing and interacting with ads so sales can time outreach. Reporting that only shows totals across the list tells you little about where to act.

Retargeting pools worth building early

Retargeting on LinkedIn reaches people who have already shown interest. It is often the cheapest place to find people ready to talk, because the audience has already filtered itself.

  • Website visitors tracked by the Insight Tag, split by the pages they viewed
  • People who watched a meaningful share of a video ad
  • People who opened a lead gen form but did not submit
  • People who engaged with your company page or event
  • Contacts from your CRM uploaded as matched audiences

A good agency sets these pools up at the start, even before they are large enough to use, so they fill while cold campaigns run. Ask which pools they would create first and how they would keep retargeting ads from repeating the same message.

Tracking setup to confirm before launch

Weak tracking makes every later decision a guess. Before the first campaign goes live, confirm these items with any agency.

  • The Insight Tag is installed on every page that matters and fires correctly
  • Conversion events are defined for the actions you care about, not just page views
  • Lead gen form submissions flow into your CRM with campaign details attached
  • UTM parameters follow a naming pattern your team can read later
  • Server side conversion sharing is considered where your setup allows it

Ask the agency to send a test lead through the whole path and show you where it lands. If nobody can trace a lead from ad to CRM record, reporting will not be trustworthy.

Offers that earn attention from B2B buyers

Targeting decides who sees the ad. The offer decides whether they act. Many LinkedIn programs fail because they ask cold audiences for a demo on the first impression.

  • Useful content: a guide, template or benchmark report that solves a real problem
  • Events: webinars or small round tables on a narrow topic
  • Assessments: an audit or review that gives the buyer something specific
  • Direct asks: a demo or call, usually best for warm or retargeted audiences

Ask each agency how it would sequence offers for your audience and who will create the assets. An agency can run excellent campaigns and still struggle if you have nothing worth offering.

Working with sales once leads arrive

LinkedIn Ads programs often stall at the handoff. Marketing reports strong lead numbers, sales says the leads are weak and nobody can prove either view. Prevent that by agreeing on definitions before launch.

  • What makes a lead worth a sales reply
  • Who replies first and through which channel
  • How sales marks a lead as accepted, rejected or booked
  • How that feedback reaches the agency so targeting can change

Remotion describes advising on how SDRs work LinkedIn leads and Sculpt describes follow up sequences synced with the CRM. Be Leaded includes follow up by email or text in the program. Whoever you choose, make sure someone owns this step by name.

Writing a brief agencies can answer well

A clear brief gets better proposals and makes them easier to compare. Keep it to one or two pages and send the same version to every firm.

  • What you sell, to whom and the typical deal size range
  • The roles and industries you want to reach
  • Any target account list and who owns it
  • Current LinkedIn spend, results and account history
  • How leads reach sales today and what happens next
  • Creative and landing page resources on your side
  • What success should look like to your leadership

Ask each firm to reply with what it would do first, what it needs from you and how it would report progress. The answers will show more about fit than any sales deck.

Switching from one LinkedIn Ads agency to another

If you are replacing a current agency, protect your history before you give notice. Confirm you are an admin on the ad account, the Insight Tag sits under your account and matched audiences, creative files and reports are stored somewhere you control.

Ask the new agency how it handles a transition. Good answers include reviewing past campaigns before changing anything, keeping working campaigns live while new ones launch and documenting what was inherited. Avoid any plan that starts by deleting what exists.

Planning ad spend without guessing

Agencies will ask about budget early and the honest answer is that the right figure depends on audience size, offer and how many campaigns you want to test at once. Rather than picking a number from a blog post, work through a few questions with each firm.

  • How large is the audience we want to reach once filters are applied?
  • How many campaigns and ad variations do you need to run for a fair test?
  • What would you cut first if spend had to drop?
  • How will spend be split between cold audiences and retargeting?

A firm that asks for far more spend than your audience can absorb or proposes testing ten ideas on a thin budget, has not thought about your situation. Look for a plan that fits your audience and leaves room to learn.

Thought leader and message ads in practice

Two formats come up often in agency pitches. Thought leader ads promote a post from a person at your company, so the ad looks like something a colleague shared. They suit companies whose founders or leaders already post useful content and are willing to have it amplified.

Message and conversation ads arrive in the LinkedIn inbox. They can work for event invitations and direct offers, but they feel intrusive if the message is generic. Ask the agency who writes these messages, how the sender is chosen and how often the same person can receive one.

Both formats depend on authentic voice. If nobody on your side can review copy written in their name, plan for that before launch.

Reporting cadence that keeps everyone honest

Agree how often you will meet and what each meeting covers. A common pattern is a short written update on a regular schedule and a longer review where marketing and sales join the agency.

  • Short updates: spend, delivery, leads and any issues that need a decision
  • Longer reviews: lead quality, meetings, pipeline and what to test next
  • Shared notes: decisions, owners and the date each change was made

Ask to see a real example of each from the agency, with client details removed. The format tells you what the firm thinks matters.

Signs an agency relationship is not working

Even a careful choice can go wrong. Watch for patterns rather than one bad stretch.

  • Reports change format whenever numbers drop
  • The same creative stays live long after it stops performing
  • Sales feedback about lead quality is acknowledged but nothing changes
  • You learn about account changes after they happen
  • Questions get answered by a different person each time

Raise concerns directly and in writing first. If nothing improves, the steps in the switching section above help you leave without losing your history.

Pairing LinkedIn Ads with other channels

LinkedIn rarely works alone. Buyers who see your ads often search your name later, visit your site or read an email from sales. Firms such as Directive, Impactable and Sculpt describe LinkedIn inside wider programs for this reason.

If you keep LinkedIn with one firm and other channels elsewhere, agree who owns the overall plan and how results are shared. Without that, each vendor claims the same deal and nobody can tell what actually moved it.

LinkedIn Ads terms you will hear on sales calls

  • Campaign Manager: LinkedIn's tool for building, running and reporting on ads
  • Insight Tag: code on your site that tracks visits and conversions from LinkedIn
  • Matched audiences: lists of contacts or companies you upload for targeting or exclusion
  • Audience expansion: a setting that lets LinkedIn show ads to people similar to your targets
  • Frequency: how many times the same person sees your ads
  • Thought leader ads: promoted posts from individual employees rather than the company page
  • Lead gen form: a form that opens inside LinkedIn with profile data filled in
  • Marketing Partner: a LinkedIn program for agencies and tools that meet its criteria
