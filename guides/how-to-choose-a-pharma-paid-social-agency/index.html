What you are actually buying

A paid social program for a prescription product is not a creative mood board plus a media invoice. You are buying a working loop: a media plan, ad units that can survive review, a media buy that respects platform rules, a way to collect the required claims and fair-balance elements plus a reporting rhythm that your internal team can stand behind.

Ask each firm to name the parts of that loop it will own. Some firms sell a broad commercialization program in which paid social is one slice. Some firms sell paid social as the main product. Both can work. The risk is hiring a firm that talks about social in general and then hands the actual ad buying to a group you never meet.

Write the scope down before you compare prices or decks. A clear scope makes later criteria easier to score.

Be specific about the product, the audience, the platforms you want to use and the pages that will receive traffic. A brief that only says "run social" leaves the firm free to invent a scope you did not mean to buy. A brief that names the review path, the claims that are in bounds and the markets that are out of bounds will produce a tighter proposal.

If more than one internal team has to live with the work, bring them into the brief. A media lead, a review lead and a brand lead often want different things from the same campaign. It is better to surface that tension before you hire than after the first ads are in review.

Why paid social for a prescription product is different

Consumer brands can often test a hook, swap a line and go live the same day. A prescription brand cannot treat paid social that way. Reviewers check that claims match the label and that fair balance travels with the benefit language under 21 CFR 202.1. Each platform also sets its own rules for drug ads and targeting. This page is not legal advice, and your reviewers decide what can run.

That difference should shape every question you ask. A firm that is strong on consumer social may still be a poor fit if it has no habit of building extra time for review, no method for keeping a rejected line from going back into a live ad set and no one who can talk with your reviewers in their language.

Ask for a recent example of a paid social unit that went through medical, legal and regulatory review. You do not need the brand name. You do need to see how comments were captured, who signed off and how long the cycle took.

Ask, too, how the firm works when a unit has to change after it is already in market. A label update, a new warning or a reviewer change can force a pause. You want a partner that can stop a live ad set quickly, swap in an approved unit and show you what ran during the overlap. A firm that has never had to do this will learn on your campaign.

How medical, legal and regulatory review should work

Review is the constraint that most often decides whether a paid social program ships. Your firm should be able to describe, in plain language, how a first draft becomes an approved unit.

Ask who sits in the review meetings. Ask how comments are logged. Ask what happens when a reviewer rejects a claim, a visual or a landing-page line after the media plan is already built. A good answer names a person, a file and a turnaround, not a slogan about quality.

Ask how the firm handles fair-balance length in formats that are short by design, such as a feed unit or a short video. If the answer is that the firm will "figure it out later," you do not yet have a working process.

Platform and FDA pages to put on the table

Two platform policy pages should be on the table in any serious conversation. Meta publishes advertising standards for drugs and pharmaceuticals. X publishes healthcare and pharmaceutical advertising rules. Your firm should be able to walk through what those pages mean for the campaign type you have in mind, including which products may need approval, which audience tools are off limits and where a unit might be rejected even after internal review.

On the regulatory side, 21 CFR 202.1 is the federal rule on prescription-drug advertising. The FDA also keeps a page for industry on the use of social media. Those documents are starting points, not a substitute for your own reviewers. This page is not legal advice. Your medical, legal and regulatory team decides what can run.

For a longer walk-through of the same rules, open our social-media advertising regulations guide for pharma teams.

A scorecard for comparing paid social firms

Give every firm the same short scorecard so a polished deck cannot hide a weak process.

Can the firm describe paid social as a distinct piece of work, not only as a line inside a wider commercialization story? Who will write the ads, who will buy the media and who will sit with your reviewers? Who will own the ad accounts, the pixels or tags and the raw export of results? How will reporting be delivered, how often and in what form? What happens in the first four to six weeks and what do you owe the firm in that window?

Keep the sheet short enough that two people on your brand team can complete it after one call. Six items you actually mark beat a long form that no one finishes.

Weight the items that would actually stop you from hiring the firm. Review process, named staff and account ownership usually belong at the top. A polished creative reel is useful, but it should not outrank a weak answer on how comments are logged or how a rejected line is kept out of a live set.

Questions worth asking before you hire

Ask for the name of the person you will speak with each week and how many other accounts that person already carries. Ask whether the people you meet in the pitch will still be writing and buying the ads after the statement of work is signed.

Ask how the firm handles a mid-flight change to the label or to a claim. Ask who pays for unused media if a unit is pulled during review. Ask whether the firm will work inside your existing ad accounts or whether it will insist on opening new ones in its own name.

Ask for a sample weekly or monthly report with the brand name removed. Look for spend, delivery, the creative that ran, the claims that were live and a plain note on what will change next. If the sample is only a vanity chart, keep looking.

The people who will touch your ads

A firm can look like the right size on paper and still be a poor fit if the people who will touch your ads are missing from the room when you buy. Ask for names, not only for practice labels.

You need someone who can walk through the media plan, someone who can walk through the claims in the units and someone who will make the edits after review. In a smaller shop those jobs may sit with two people. In a larger shop they may sit with a wider group. Either setup can work if you can reach the people doing the work without a long chain of account notes.

If a firm will not name the account team until after you sign, treat that as missing information, not as a detail to settle later.

Ownership of ad accounts and campaign data

Before you start, agree in writing who owns the ad accounts, the pages or handles, the pixels or tags and the export of results. If the firm buys media only inside accounts it controls, you may have trouble moving the work later or showing a reviewer the exact settings that were used.

The same point applies to audiences and to creative files. You should walk away with the ads, the comments from review and a raw export of delivery and spend. A slide deck does not replace that file.

Ask how access is given and taken back. Ask how long it takes to add a reviewer or an internal media lead to the account.

What useful reporting looks like for a brand team

Reporting for a prescription brand has to do more than show clicks. It has to show what ran, which claims were in market, how much was spent, what the platforms delivered and what you will change next. Your internal team should be able to carry that report into a review meeting without having to rewrite it.

Set the cadence before the first ads run. Weekly is common while a campaign is being tuned. A monthly pack may be enough once the loop is stable. In either case, the report should land on a date you can plan around, not whenever someone has time to export a chart.

If a firm cannot show you a redacted report from similar work, you are being asked to buy a process you have not seen.

The shape of a first paid social engagement

A first paid social engagement should have a visible shape. In the opening weeks the firm should confirm the product, the audience, the claims that can be used, the platform or platforms and the review path. After that it should return with a plan, a set of ad units and a timeline that includes review, not one that treats review as a short courtesy pass.

Only after those pieces exist should media start. If a firm wants to launch in a few days without a review plan, it is not ready for this category.

Ask what you must provide in the first two weeks: a label, a claims grid, a landing page, access to accounts and a named reviewer. If that list is missing, the start date you are being sold is not real.

Write the first-engagement plan so that both sides can see the dependencies. If review will take a week, the media start date has to move with it. If a landing page is still in legal, the ads that point to it should not be set to run. A plan that ignores those dependencies will look fast in a pitch and stall in week two.

When a firm is probably the wrong hire

Pause the process if the firm cannot name the people who will do the work. Pause if it will not say who owns the ad accounts. Pause if it has no answer for fair balance in short formats. Pause if the only social work it can show is for a consumer brand with no review trail.

Be wary when a firm sells a very large commercialization stack and treats paid social as a small add-on it cannot describe in detail. The wider platform may be strong. Your need may still be a paid social program with a tight review loop. Those are different purchases.

Be wary, too, when a firm talks only about reach and "going viral." Paid social for a prescription brand is a controlled media buy. Excitement is not a plan.

A mismatch on culture can also be a reason to pause. If your reviewers need calm, documented comments and the firm wants to move fast and fix things later, the relationship will wear out during the first cycle. Paid social for a prescription brand rewards a firm that can be both quick in production and careful in review, not one that treats those as opposites.

Frequently asked questions

What should I look for first when I choose a pharma paid social agency?

Start with the review loop, account ownership and the named people who will write and buy the ads. If a firm cannot explain those three points, it is not ready to run paid social for a prescription brand, even if the rest of the deck looks strong.

Can Be Leaded work alongside a large commercialization firm I already use?

It can be. Some teams keep a large firm for a wider commercial program and hire Be Leaded to plan and run paid social. If that split would create two review paths or two sets of ad accounts, sort that out before any media runs.

Do I need a firm that only works in healthcare?

You need a firm that can work with your reviewers and with platform rules for pharmaceuticals. Some healthcare-only firms do that well. Some paid social teams do it well too. Judge the process you are buying, not only the category language on the firm homepage.

Which documents should I read before I brief a firm?

Read the Meta advertising standards for drugs and pharmaceuticals, the X healthcare and pharmaceutical advertising rules, 21 CFR 202.1 and your own label plus claims grid. Bring your reviewers into the brief so the firm is not guessing what can be said.

How long should I allow for review before ads go live?

Build review into the first-engagement plan, not as a short pass at the end. The exact number of days depends on your product, the number of units and how your reviewers work. Ask each firm how it has handled that cycle and what it needs from you in the first two weeks.

Who should own the ad accounts?

You should leave with access to the ad accounts, the pages or handles, the pixels or tags and a raw export of results. If a firm will only buy media inside accounts it controls, get a written plan for moving that work back under your login.

Tell us who you need to reach and how review currently works for your ads. We will say honestly whether we should run the paid social work. Month to month, no long-term contract.
