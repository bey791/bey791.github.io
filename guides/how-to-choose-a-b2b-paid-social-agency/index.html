Who this selection guide is written for

This guide is for a marketing lead, a founder or a head of demand at a business that sells B2B and has decided to bring in an outside firm for paid social. You may have run a few LinkedIn campaigns yourself. You may have a Meta account that a previous contractor left behind. Either way the question on your desk is the same: which firm do we hire and how do we avoid paying for six months of learning on our money?

It is not a guide to running the channel. Campaign structure, offers and funnel stages belong in a strategy guide. It is also not a ranked list. If you want a shorter snapshot of named firms first, our list of the best B2B paid social firms covers that, then you can come back here for the evaluation work.

What you will get here is a way to define the job, a short brief you can send, a scorecard, the questions that separate firms in a first call and a set of firms described only from what their own websites say.

Decide what a good month looks like before you talk to anyone

Most bad hires start with a vague goal. A firm is told to "grow pipeline" and then reports on whatever number moved. Before you speak to a single vendor, write down the result you would be happy to see in a normal month once the program is running. Be concrete about the unit: meetings booked with the right job titles, demo requests from companies in your target list, sales accepted opportunities or trial starts from a named segment.

Then write down what you would accept in the first month, when audiences are still being tested. Firms that hear both numbers will either tell you the plan is realistic or tell you why it is not. Firms that only hear "more leads" will simply aim for more leads.

Finally, write down who on your side owns each lead the minute it arrives. A B2B paid social program that produces a form fill at 4 PM on a Friday and gets a reply on Tuesday is not a media problem. It is a follow up problem. You want a firm that asks about it in the first call.

Meta, LinkedIn and X do different jobs for a B2B buyer

The three platforms are not interchangeable. A firm that treats them as one budget line is showing you a warning sign. LinkedIn lets you target by job title, seniority, company size and named company lists. That precision costs more per click, so it suits deals where one right meeting is worth a lot.

Meta reaches the same people outside work hours at a lower cost per impression. It works well for B2B offers that can be explained in one screen, for retargeting people who already visited your site and for lookalike audiences built from a customer list. X can be useful when your buyers follow specific industry conversations or events and when you want to reach people who engage with named accounts or topics.

Ask each firm which platform it would start on for your buyer and why. A good answer names the platform, the audience, the offer and the reason in one breath. A weak answer says all three, from day one, with an even split.

The five kinds of firm you will meet

Firms that sell B2B paid social fall into a few broad shapes. Knowing which shape you are talking to explains most of what is in the proposal.

  • Single platform shops. They run one channel, often LinkedIn, in depth. Good when your buyer clearly lives on that platform. Weaker when you need Meta retargeting or creative across channels.
  • SaaS growth teams. They sell paid media plus landing pages, creative and reporting to software companies, often with a playbook for trials and demos. Good fit for SaaS. Less useful if you sell services or a long consultative deal.
  • Full B2B marketing firms. Paid social is one service among brand, content, web and RevOps. Useful if you want one partner for everything. Paid social may get less senior attention.
  • Freelancers and small collectives. Lower cost and direct access to the person doing the work. Capacity and cover for holidays are the usual risks.
  • Ads plus follow up firms. They run the ads and also contact each lead quickly by email, text or phone so the lead becomes a meeting. Be Leaded is in this group.

None of these is better in general. The right shape depends on your offer, your sales team and how much of the work you can do yourself.

Check your own side before you brief a firm

A firm can only work with what you give it. Before you send a brief, check five things on your side. If two or more are missing, fix them first or make them part of the scope.

  • A one sentence offer that your sales team would say the same way.
  • A landing page or lead form you can change in days, not weeks.
  • A CRM that records where each lead came from and what happened to it.
  • A named person who replies to new leads the same day.
  • Enough budget committed for long enough to test more than one audience.

Being honest here saves money. A firm that has to rebuild your tracking in month one is not failing at paid social. It is doing setup that you should have priced into the plan.

Write a one page brief that firms can quote against

A short brief makes proposals comparable. Send the same page to every firm on your list and ask them to answer it in writing before any pitch meeting. Keep it to one page so the answers stay focused.

  • Who you sell to: industries, company size, job titles and any named account list.
  • What you sell and what the next step is for a buyer: a demo, a call, a trial or a quote.
  • Platforms in scope: Meta, LinkedIn and X.
  • Current state: accounts that exist, past results you trust and assets you already have.
  • Who follows up on leads and how fast.
  • Monthly ad budget range and the fee range you can approve.
  • How you will judge the first quarter.

Ask each firm to reply with its first ninety day plan, the named people on your account, the fee and what the fee includes. Anything they will not put in writing at this stage is unlikely to be clearer later.

A scorecard for comparing firms side by side

Score each firm from one to five on the criteria below, with your own weights. Score from the written reply first, then adjust after the call. Writing the score before the call keeps a polished presenter from winning on charm alone.

  • Fit with your buyer. Have they worked with companies that sell to the same job titles and deal sizes?
  • Platform judgment. Do they recommend a starting platform for a reason or every platform by default?
  • Creative process. Who writes the ads, how many versions get tested and how often they change.
  • After the click. Do they build or improve the landing page and the form and do they ask about follow up?
  • Reporting. Do they report on meetings and opportunities from your CRM or only on platform metrics?
  • People. Who works on your account each week and how senior are they?
  • Fee clarity. Is it clear what the fee covers and what costs extra?
  • Ownership and exit. Do you own the ad accounts and data and how easy is it to leave?

Two firms with the same total score can still be very different hires. Look at where each one scored low and ask whether you can live with that gap.

How to read a proposal line by line

Proposals tend to look alike on the first page. The useful detail is further in. Read each one with a pen and look for four things.

First, the scope list. Count how many items describe your outcome and how many describe the firm's activity. "Weekly optimization" is activity. "Meetings from target accounts reported weekly from your CRM" is closer to an outcome.

Second, the first ninety days. A credible plan names what gets built in the first weeks, which audiences and offers get tested first and when the first real read on results is expected. A plan that claims full speed in week one usually skips setup.

Third, the people page. Match names to roles and ask how many other accounts each person runs. Fourth, the contract terms: notice period, minimum term, who owns what and what happens to the ad accounts if you part ways.

Questions to ask on the first call

Use the call to test judgment, not to hear the pitch again. These questions tend to show how a firm thinks.

  • If you could only run one platform for us for the first two months, which one and why?
  • Which audience would you test first and what result would make you stop testing it?
  • Walk us through what happens in the first hour after someone fills in our form.
  • Which number will you show us every week and where does it come from?
  • Who writes our ads and how many new ads do we see in a normal month?
  • What would you need from our team each week to do this well?
  • What would make you tell us to pause spending?
  • If we stop working together, what do we keep on the last day?

Write the answers down. The firm you hire will be held to them and having them in writing helps at the first quarterly review.

Answers that should make you pause

Some answers are a quiet sign that the fit is wrong. None of them is a dealbreaker alone, but two or three together usually are.

  • Every platform is recommended from day one, with no reason given for the split.
  • Results are described only in clicks, impressions or cost per lead, with no mention of meetings or opportunities.
  • The firm has no view on what happens after the form or says follow up is your problem.
  • The people on the call will not be the people on your account.
  • The firm wants to run ads only inside accounts it owns.
  • A long minimum term is required before any results are in.

A good firm will often say something you did not want to hear, such as "your budget is too thin for LinkedIn alone" or "fix the landing page before we spend more." That kind of candor is worth more than an easy yes.

Creative is where most B2B paid social is won or lost

Targeting on LinkedIn and Meta is available to everyone. What separates programs is the ad itself: the hook, the offer and whether a busy buyer understands it in two seconds. Ask each firm to show how it plans creative for a buyer like yours, not a polished reel from another industry.

Find out who writes the copy, who designs the visuals and how often new versions go live. Ask how they decide that an ad is tired and needs replacing. Ask whether they test different messages for different people in the buying group, such as a technical evaluator and a budget holder.

Also ask how approvals work on your side. If your team needs three days to sign off each ad, the firm needs to plan for that and you need to know whether the fee assumes a faster cycle.

Lead forms, landing pages and the first hour after the click

Platform lead forms on LinkedIn and Meta are quick to fill in, which can raise volume and lower quality. A landing page asks more of the buyer but can qualify them better. A good firm will have a view on which to use for your offer and will test both when the answer is unclear.

Then ask what happens next. The longer a new lead waits for a reply, the more likely it is to go cold. Ask the firm whether it connects forms to your CRM, whether it sends the first reply itself and how a lead reaches a salesperson's calendar.

Many paid social proposals leave this step out. It is the reason Be Leaded runs ads and follow up together.

Reporting your sales leader will trust

Platform dashboards report what the platform can see. Your sales leader cares about what happened in the CRM. A firm you can trust will connect the two and show you, at least monthly, how many leads became meetings, how many meetings became opportunities and what each of those cost.

Ask to see a sample report with the client details removed. Check that it shows spend by platform, results by audience, the ads that ran and the step from lead to meeting. If the report stops at cost per lead, ask how they would extend it. If they cannot, you will be doing that work yourself.

Agree in writing which numbers come from the platforms and which come from your CRM, so that monthly reviews argue about decisions rather than definitions.

Fees, ad spend and what the invoice should show

Fees in this market come in a few shapes: a flat monthly fee, a fee tied to a share of ad spend, a fee per deliverable or a mix of those with a setup charge. None of them is wrong. What matters is that you can see what the fee pays for and how it would change if your budget grew.

Ad spend should be paid to the platforms from accounts you own, so you can see every charge. Ask whether creative, landing pages and reporting are inside the fee or billed separately. Ask how the fee changes during a month with no ad spend.

Be careful with comparisons across firms. A lower fee that excludes creative and landing pages can cost more in practice than a higher fee that includes them.

Ad accounts, data and a clean exit

You should own your LinkedIn Campaign Manager account, your Meta business assets, your X ads account, your pixels and your audiences. The firm should work inside them with access you grant and can remove. This sounds basic, but plenty of companies find out at the end of a contract that their history sits in someone else's account.

Ask for an exit plan before you sign: what is handed over, in what format and how fast. A firm that is confident in its work will not mind writing that down. It also makes it easier to switch later if the fit changes as you grow.

Start with a short first phase

Instead of committing to a long engagement on the strength of a pitch, agree a first phase with a clear scope and a review at the end. That phase should include setup, a first round of audience and offer tests and a written read on what worked.

Set the review date before you start. Agree in advance what you will look at on that date and what result would lead you to continue, change course or stop. A firm that is comfortable with that structure is usually one that expects to earn the next month.

What the first quarter with a new firm should look like

A realistic first quarter has three parts. The first weeks are setup: access to accounts, tracking checked, forms connected to the CRM, audiences built and the first ads approved. Expect few results in this stretch.

The middle weeks are testing. Several audiences and messages run side by side and the firm should tell you which ones are cut and why. This is when weekly notes matter most.

The last weeks are the first honest read. You should be able to see which platform, audience and offer produce meetings, what they cost and what the firm proposes for the next quarter. If you cannot see that by then, ask why before you renew.

Using the scorecard on the firms above

Run every name through the same brief and scorecard. A LinkedIn focused shop will likely score high on platform depth and lower on Meta retargeting. A SaaS growth team may score high on landing pages and reporting but may not suit a services company. A firm with a stated minimum spend is simply not an option if your budget sits below it.

Keep two or three firms for calls. More than that and the comparison blurs. Fewer and you lose the contrast that shows what a strong answer sounds like.

Mistakes buyers make when they pick a firm

  • Choosing on the creative reel instead of on how the firm would reach your buyer.
  • Comparing fees without checking what each fee includes.
  • Hiring for LinkedIn because it is B2B, without checking whether Meta reaches the same people for less.
  • Leaving follow up out of scope and then blaming the ads for poor meetings.
  • Signing a long minimum term before a first phase has been reviewed.
  • Letting the firm own the ad accounts.

Each of these is easy to avoid once you know to look for it. The brief, the scorecard and the questions above are designed to catch them early.

Frequently asked questions

How do I choose a B2B paid social agency?

Define the result you want in a normal month, send every firm the same one page brief, score the written replies on buyer fit, platform judgment, creative, follow up, reporting, people, fees and ownership, then run a short first phase with the best two or three before you commit.

Which platforms should a B2B paid social firm run?

Usually Meta, LinkedIn and X, chosen for your buyer. LinkedIn targets by job title and company. Meta often reaches the same people at a lower cost and suits retargeting. X can help when buyers follow specific industry conversations.

Should I hire a LinkedIn only firm?

It can make sense when your buyers are narrow, senior and easy to find by job title. If your offer is simple to explain or you need retargeting, a firm that also runs Meta may get more meetings from the same budget.

What should I ask a firm on the first call?

Ask which platform they would start on and why, what happens in the first hour after a lead arrives, which weekly number they report and where it comes from, who writes the ads and what you keep if you part ways.

Who should own the ad accounts?

Your company should. The firm should work inside your LinkedIn, Meta and X ad accounts with access you control, so the history, audiences and pixels stay with you if the engagement ends.

How should a firm report results?

On meetings and opportunities from your CRM as well as platform metrics. A monthly report should show spend by platform, results by audience, the ads that ran and the step from lead to meeting.

Does Be Leaded run B2B paid social?

Yes. For B2B buyers, our team runs paid social on Meta, LinkedIn and X. We also set up the page and the form behind each ad, then reply to every lead within minutes so more of them turn into booked meetings. Month to month, no long-term contract.

How much does Be Leaded charge?

Which paid social partners are known for turning advertising spend into measurable growth?

Look for a partner that ties ad spend to leads, meetings and pipeline in your own CRM, not to clicks or reach. Ask for a sample report before you sign and keep the ad accounts in your name. Be Leaded runs paid social on Meta, LinkedIn and X, and you can ask for a sample report on the first call.
