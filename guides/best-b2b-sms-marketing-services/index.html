The short answer

There is no single best B2B SMS marketing service, because B2B teams buy texting for different jobs. A sales team that wants to text leads from inside its CRM needs something different from a marketing team sending event reminders to subscribers and both need something different from a product team wiring texts into an app.

What the strong options share is easy to list. They register your sending numbers with the carriers, they capture and store consent, they handle STOP replies without manual work and they give your team a shared place to read and answer replies. The differences are in where the texting lives (a standalone app, your CRM or your own code) and in how much help you get setting it up.

This guide compares ten platforms in alphabetical order, using only what each vendor says on its own site and explains where a managed service such as Be Leaded fits for teams that want texting run as part of outbound rather than bought as another tool.

Who this comparison is for

This comparison is aimed at B2B companies that already reach buyers by email and want text to do more of the work once a buyer is engaged. Typical readers run a sales team that books demos, a marketing team that runs events or webinars, a customer success team with renewals to manage or a founder who handles all three.

It is not a guide to consumer text blasts. Retail promotions, coupon clubs and abandoned cart flows have their own tools and their own playbooks. Some vendors below serve both worlds and we note where a product leans toward consumer marketing so you can judge the fit.

If you are still deciding whether texting belongs in your outreach at all, start with our guide to cold SMS versus cold email and come back here when you are ready to pick a vendor.

Three kinds of SMS service for B2B teams

Most confusion in this category comes from comparing products that do different jobs. Sorting vendors into three groups makes the shortlist shorter.

  • Texting platforms with a dashboard: web apps where your team builds lists, sends campaigns and answers replies; examples below include EZ Texting, SimpleTexting, Textline and TextMagic
  • CRM centered texting tools: products built to live inside HubSpot, Salesforce or an applicant tracking system so reps text from the record they already work in; Heymarket, Salesmsg and TextUs sit closest to this group and ActiveCampaign brings texting into its own marketing and CRM platform
  • Messaging APIs: infrastructure your developers call from code, with dashboards for monitoring rather than for writing campaigns; Twilio and ClickSend sit in this group and ClickSend offers both a dashboard and an API

A fourth option sits outside the software market entirely: a managed service that runs texting for you as part of a wider program. That is the model Be Leaded uses and it suits teams that want the outcome without adding another tool to manage.

ActiveCampaign

ActiveCampaign is a marketing automation platform best known for email. Its SMS marketing page presents texting as a channel that works alongside email, with the same contact data shared between the two so campaigns can switch channels without a separate list.

  • Two way messaging with an SMS inbox for replies
  • A dedicated ten digit long code with in app registration
  • Consent ready opt in forms plus a quiet hours setting
  • SMS automations that coordinate with email and WhatsApp
  • Integrations listed on the page include Salesforce, Calendly and Zapier

Best fit: B2B teams that already run email nurture in ActiveCampaign and want texting inside the same journeys rather than in a separate tool.

Worth checking: how SMS is added to your plan, which countries you need to reach and whether the inbox suits sales conversations as well as marketing replies.

Next to cold email: if your cold email runs in a separate sending tool, the cleanest pattern is to move a contact into ActiveCampaign only after they reply and agree to texts, so the SMS journey starts with consent already recorded. Keep cold sending out of the marketing account that holds your opted in subscribers.

ClickSend

ClickSend sells business SMS and MMS through a web app, integrations and an API. Its US site describes sending and receiving texts in the United States and internationally, from a handful of messages to large campaigns, with the same account reachable from a dashboard or from code.

  • MMS for images and sound alongside text
  • Rich messaging options including RCS, Facebook Messenger and WhatsApp Business
  • A marketplace of integrations with business apps for no code automation
  • Chat support around the clock, according to the site

Best fit: teams that want one account for both a simple sending dashboard and an API, with integrations doing the work of moving contacts in and out. Be Leaded uses it for this reason.

Worth checking: how replies reach the people who need to answer them, how consent records are stored and which integrations match your CRM.

Next to cold email: contacts who agree to texts can be pushed into ClickSend through an integration or the API once the email reply is tagged, which keeps texting separate from the cold sending setup. This is the pattern we run: email earns the reply, the reply grants permission and ClickSend carries the scheduling texts that follow.

Next to cold email: treat EZ Texting as the place for buyers who have already opted in, such as webinar registrants or customers. Feed it from a form with clear text consent rather than from an outbound list. Use its workflows for reminders around events or demos.

Worth checking: how team seats and inboxes are structured and whether the email features meet your needs or sit alongside your existing email tool.

Next to cold email: because replies land in a shared inbox tied to the CRM, a rep can move a thread from email to text without losing context. Set a rule that a contact enters a texting campaign only after a recorded yes. Let the shared inbox carry the one to one conversation from there.

Next to cold email: developers can build the exact handoff you want, such as a webhook that creates a texting thread when an email reply is tagged as ready to meet. That control comes with ownership: someone has to maintain the build and the registration over time.

How to choose: match the service to the job

Start with the job you need texting to do, not with a feature list. The same product can be the right answer for one team and the wrong one for another.

  • Reps texting leads from the CRM: look first at tools that live inside your CRM, such as Salesmsg, Heymarket or TextUs
  • Marketing sends to an opted in list: look at dashboard platforms with opt in tools and automation, such as EZ Texting or SimpleTexting
  • Email nurture plus texting: if your email already runs in ActiveCampaign, its SMS add on keeps both channels in one journey
  • A busy inbound inbox: Textline and Heymarket focus on routing and shared replies
  • Several countries: TextMagic and ClickSend describe international sending; check each market you need
  • Texting built into your product: an API such as Twilio or ClickSend gives developers the control they need
  • Outbound with texting after a reply: a managed program such as Be Leaded covers the email that earns the reply and the texts that follow

If two vendors look equal, pick the one whose inbox your team will actually open. Unanswered replies cost more than any difference in features.

Questions to ask every vendor in a demo

  • Will you submit and manage our A2P 10DLC brand and campaign registration and what do you need from us?
  • Where is consent stored for each contact and can we export it with the source and date?
  • How are STOP and HELP replies handled and do opt outs sync back to our CRM?
  • Can several people answer from one number and can we assign threads?
  • Which CRM fields sync in each direction and how fast?
  • Can scheduled messages follow each recipient's time zone?
  • What happens to our numbers and message history if we leave?
  • Which countries can we text from this account and with which number types?

Ask each vendor to show these live rather than describe them. A five minute walkthrough of the opt out flow tells you more than a feature grid.

Consent features matter more than campaign features

Every vendor above mentions consent, registration or opt out handling on its own site. We only text after a buyer agrees. This page is not legal advice, so confirm current rules with your counsel.

We cover how to collect consent from business buyers, what the first text should say and how to run a pilot in our B2B SMS marketing playbook. In a vendor comparison, the practical test is simpler: can you show, for any number you text, where and when the person agreed?

How an SMS service fits with cold email

None of the platforms above is designed to replace cold email as a first touch for B2B outreach in the US and several say plainly that their tools are for contacts who opted in. The pattern that works is a handoff: email or LinkedIn starts the conversation and texting takes over once the buyer agrees.

That handoff is where most teams struggle. The email tool and the texting tool are usually separate, so a rep has to notice a reply, ask for permission to text, record the answer and move the thread. Look for a texting service that can receive contacts from your email or CRM workflow without manual exports and decide in advance who owns the handoff.

If you would rather not build that handoff yourself, a managed program can run both sides. That is the gap Be Leaded fills: the outbound email, the reply handling and the texting after a yes.

How SMS services price their plans

We leave prices out of this guide because they change often and depend on volume, number type and country. The pricing models themselves are worth understanding before you compare quotes.

  • Usage based: pay for what you send with no subscription, the model Twilio describes on its messaging page
  • Pay as you go credit: buy credit and top up, the model TextMagic describes
  • Volume based plans: TextUs says its pricing follows message volume rather than seats
  • Platform add ons: texting added to a wider marketing or CRM plan, as with ActiveCampaign
  • Managed programs: a monthly fee for a service that includes the work, as with Be Leaded

Beyond the plan, ask about registration fees, carrier fees passed through to you, costs for extra numbers and charges for MMS. Ask for an estimate based on your real volumes and countries rather than the headline plan.

Warning signs when comparing SMS services

  • An offer to text purchased lists: any vendor or agency that suggests this is asking you to carry the risk
  • Vague answers on registration: if a vendor cannot explain who registers your brand and campaign, expect filtered messages
  • No export of consent records: you should be able to take your proof of consent with you
  • Replies that land nowhere: a two way channel with no clear owner frustrates buyers
  • Long commitments before a trial: texting is easy to pilot, so a vendor should let you test a narrow use case first

None of these warning signs apply to a specific vendor above. They are the questions that separate a tool you can rely on from one that creates work later.

A sensible way to trial a shortlist

Pick two vendors that match the job you defined and run the same narrow test in each. Meeting reminders for booked calls are a good test because every message has a clear purpose and consent is easy to collect at booking.

  • Register a number and note how much of the work the vendor did for you
  • Add an optional mobile field with plain consent wording to your booking page
  • Send confirmations and reminders for a few weeks
  • Have the person who answers replies rate each inbox
  • Check that opt outs and consent records flowed into your CRM

Choose the vendor your team found easier to run day to day. Then repeat the same checks on a second use case, for example follow up after an inbound request.

Which teams use B2B SMS services and for what

The vendor you pick should match the team that will own texting day to day. Here is how the work usually splits inside a B2B company.

Sales development and account executives

Reps use text to settle meeting times, send a calendar link after a buyer agrees and nudge a prospect who went quiet after saying yes. What they need most is texting next to the CRM record so the history stays in one place. A tool that forces them into a separate app tends to go unused within weeks.

Marketing and events

Marketing teams text registrants about webinars, send reminders on the day of an event and share booth details at trade shows. They need opt in tools, lists, scheduling and reporting more than a shared inbox. Consent for these messages usually comes from a registration form, so the form wording matters as much as the platform.

Customer success and account management

Success teams use text for onboarding steps, renewal heads ups and quick check ins after a support ticket closes. They need threads assigned to the right owner and a record that shows the customer asked for texts. Shared inbox products tend to suit this team best.

Recruiting and staffing

Staffing firms text candidates about interviews and shifts. Several vendors in this list mention recruiting on their own sites, with integrations into applicant tracking systems. If your company both recruits and sells, check whether one platform can serve both teams with separate numbers.

Setting up the handoff from email to text

Whichever vendor you choose, the moment a thread moves from email to text is where programs most often break. A clean handoff has four parts.

  • A trigger: a positive reply in email that someone tags as ready to meet
  • An ask: a short line offering to settle the time by text, with the buyer choosing the number
  • A record: the reply that granted permission saved against the contact, with the date
  • A route: the contact created or updated in the texting tool without a manual export

Test the route before you launch. Send a reply to yourself, grant permission and check that the contact, the consent note and the first text all appear where your team expects them. If any step needs a copy and paste, it will be skipped on a busy day.

Write the handoff down as a one page playbook. Include the exact wording of the permission ask, who tags replies, how fast a text should follow and what happens when a buyer says stop. New team members can then run it the same way on their first day.

SMS terms buyers meet in vendor demos

  • A2P: application to person messaging, meaning texts sent by a business system rather than typed on a personal phone
  • 10DLC: a standard ten digit local number registered for business texting in the US
  • Toll free texting: sending from a toll free number, which has its own verification process
  • Short code: a five or six digit number used for high volume programs that need their own approval
  • MMS: messages that carry images, audio or video
  • RCS: a richer messaging format on supported phones with branding and interactive elements
  • Two way texting: recipients can reply and a person can answer in the same thread
  • Shared inbox: one place where several team members read and answer texts
  • Keyword opt in: a person joins a list by texting a word to your number
  • Double opt in: a second confirmation step after someone first signs up

Knowing these terms makes demos faster. When a vendor says it supports a number type, ask which registration it handles and which it leaves to you.

Questions to ask a managed SMS or outbound service

If you are weighing a managed program instead of a platform, the questions change. You are buying judgment and work, not features.

  • Who owns the contact list, the sending numbers and the reply history if we stop?
  • How do you decide when a buyer has given permission to text?
  • Will you ever text numbers that did not opt in or reply? The only acceptable answer is no.
  • Which texting platform do you use and who registers the numbers?
  • How do replies reach our team when a buyer wants to talk now?
  • What are the terms and can we leave after any month?

Mistakes B2B teams make when choosing an SMS service

  • Buying for campaigns when the need is conversations: a broadcast tool frustrates reps who need to answer one buyer at a time
  • Skipping the owner question: no tool helps if nobody is assigned to answer texts during working hours
  • Ignoring the CRM: texts that never reach the record leave the next rep without context
  • Treating registration as paperwork: an honest campaign description is what carriers check against your real messages
  • Comparing headline prices: the real cost depends on volume, number types, countries and fees beyond the plan
  • Starting too wide: launching every use case at once makes it hard to see which one helps

Each of these mistakes is cheap to avoid before you sign and expensive to fix after. A narrow pilot with a named owner catches most of them in the first few weeks.

A shortlist worksheet you can copy

Write down your answers before you book demos. Vendors then have to respond to your situation instead of their script.

  • Main job for texting: scheduling, reminders, campaigns, support or alerts
  • Who answers replies: a named person or team and their hours
  • Where texting should live: CRM, a separate inbox or your own product
  • How consent is collected: form, email reply, event sign up or keyword
  • Countries and number types: where your buyers are and which numbers you need
  • Systems to connect: CRM, email tool, calendar and help desk
  • Build or buy the work: run it yourselves or hand it to a managed program

Score each shortlisted vendor against these lines after the demo. The one that fits the most lines without workarounds is usually the right choice, whatever its position in any list.

How B2B SMS differs from consumer text marketing

Most SMS platforms grew up serving retailers, restaurants and local services. Their homepages show coupon codes, flash sales and cart reminders. B2B texting uses the same pipes for a different purpose. That changes what you should look for.

  • Fewer recipients, more value each: a B2B program may text a few hundred buyers rather than a large subscriber list, so per message features matter less than inbox quality
  • Conversations over campaigns: most B2B texts are one to one, about a meeting or an account, rather than one message to many
  • Several people per account: a buying group may include a champion, a manager and a finance contact, each with their own consent
  • Longer cycles: a thread may pause for weeks while a deal moves, so history has to stay attached to the account
  • CRM as the source of truth: B2B teams judge texting by meetings and renewals recorded in the CRM, not by clicks

A platform built for consumer blasts can still work for B2B if its inbox and CRM sync are strong. Test those parts first, because they are where B2B use differs most.

What B2B teams text at each stage

  • Before a meeting: confirming the time, sending the calendar link and a reminder on the day, once the buyer agreed to texts
  • After a meeting: a short thank you and the one next step, with detail kept in email
  • During evaluation: quick answers on timing or access while documents travel by email
  • At signature: a heads up that paperwork has been sent, if the buyer prefers text for that
  • After the sale: onboarding steps, service notices and renewal reminders for customers who asked for them

Notice that nothing on this list is a first touch. Every stage starts after the buyer engaged and agreed. That is the line we hold in our own programs.

When a platform is enough and when it is not

A platform is enough when you already have a steady flow of engaged buyers and someone with time to own texting. Inbound demo requests, event registrations and existing customers all fit that description. Pick the vendor that matches the job and run it in house.

A platform is not enough when the real problem is too few conversations. No texting tool creates replies on its own. In the US it should not be used to start them with strangers. If your pipeline needs more first conversations, the work starts with outbound email and LinkedIn. Texting is the layer that speeds things up afterward.

That is the case for a managed program. It covers the outreach that earns replies and the texting that follows, under one plan and one owner.

Measuring whether texting helps your pipeline

Vendors report delivery and reply figures, but those numbers do not tell you whether texting moved deals. Decide what you will measure before the pilot starts so the result is not a matter of opinion.

  • Show rate: the share of booked meetings that happen, compared with a period or group without text reminders
  • Time to book: the days between a positive reply and a meeting on the calendar
  • Opt out rate: how many people ask to stop, which tells you whether the messages feel welcome
  • Rep time: the hours your team spends in the texting tool each week
  • Pipeline created: opportunities that include a text thread in their history

Compare like with like. Run reminders for half of new meetings and none for the other half over the same weeks. If show rate and time to book improve without a rise in opt outs, texting is earning its place. If nothing moves, the problem is likely earlier in the funnel.
