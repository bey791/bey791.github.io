How to run buyer outreach for a business listing, step by step

Sell-side buyer outreach for a broker listing means building a list of likely buyers, sending each one a blind teaser, signing an NDA before any detail goes out, then following up and qualifying until the right buyers are talking to the seller. Brokers run it in house or outsource it to a partner such as Be Leaded, which runs buyer outreach for brokers under the broker's name.

Below are the steps in order, with the checks that keep a quiet listing quiet.

What buyer outreach means on a broker listing

Most listings attract some inbound interest. A posting on a marketplace brings inquiries, a broker's own database turns up a few repeat buyers and a referral or two may arrive from a banker or an accountant. Buyer outreach is the other half. It is the deliberate work of deciding who should hear about a business, reaching those people one by one and moving the serious ones toward a conversation with the seller.

On the sell side, outreach serves one goal: enough qualified interest at the same time that the seller has real choices. A single interested party gives the seller little leverage. Several credible buyers looking at once tend to sharpen terms, shorten debates over price and protect the deal if one buyer walks away during diligence.

Outreach is also where most confidentiality risk lives. Every note that leaves the office is a chance for the wrong person to learn that a business is for sale. A good process is careful about who hears what and when, and it treats the seller's privacy as part of the job rather than a box to tick.

The process at a glance

Every broker adapts the order to the listing, but the core sequence looks much the same from one deal to the next.

  • Prepare the listing: confirm the seller's goals, the deal story and what may be shared before an NDA
  • Build the buyer universe: research strategic acquirers, private equity groups, independent sponsors, search funds and individual buyers
  • Score and tier: rank each name on fit, capacity and likelihood
  • Get seller approval: walk the seller through the names before anyone is contacted
  • Send the blind teaser: a short anonymous profile with no name, address or identifying detail
  • Run the NDA flow: screen the request, sign the NDA, then release the full memorandum
  • Follow up on a set cadence: across email, LinkedIn and the phone
  • Qualify and track: confirm funds, experience and intent, then log every touch in one place

The sections below cover each step in detail. If you want the wider picture of where buyers come from, including listing sites and standing databases, read our guide on how business brokers find buyers. This page stays focused on the outreach itself.

Get the listing ready before the first note

Outreach goes badly when it starts before the broker and the seller agree on the basics. Before you write a single message, settle four things with the seller.

  • The seller's goals. A full exit, a partial sale, a transition period or a role after closing all point to different buyers.
  • Who must not hear. Competitors, key customers, suppliers and sometimes employees can be off limits, at least in the first wave.
  • What can be said blind. Industry, region, size range and a few strengths, worded so nobody can guess the company.
  • What waits for the NDA. The name, the financials, the customer list and anything else that identifies the business.

Have the confidential information memorandum, or at least a solid draft, ready before outreach begins. Buyers who sign an NDA expect the next document quickly. A long gap between a signed NDA and the memorandum cools interest and makes the process look disorganized.

Agree on the deal story too. One or two sentences on why the business is attractive and why the owner is selling now will shape every note, every call and every answer to the first question a buyer asks.

Build the buyer universe

The buyer universe is the full research list of everyone who might plausibly buy the business. Build it wide first and narrow it later. Cutting names too early is one of the most common reasons a listing runs out of buyers before it finds the right one.

Pull names from industry directories, trade association member lists, acquisition announcements in the sector, private equity portfolio pages, search fund networks and your own past deals. For each name, record why it might fit. A list without reasons is hard to score and even harder to defend when the seller asks why a firm is on it.

Most broker listings draw from five buyer groups. Each one reads a teaser differently and needs a slightly different approach.

Strategic acquirers

Strategic acquirers are operating companies that buy businesses in their own industry or a nearby one. They may want new customers, a new region, a product line or a team with skills they lack. Look at direct competitors in other regions, companies in adjacent services and suppliers or customers who might want to move up or down the chain.

Strategics often understand the business fastest, but they also carry the most confidentiality risk because many of them compete with the seller. Reach the decision maker directly, usually the owner, the CEO or someone in corporate development. A generic inbox rarely gets a listing in front of the person who decides.

Private equity groups and add-on buyers

Private equity firms buy as a platform, meaning a company they build around, or as an add-on bolted onto a business they already own. For many broker listings the add-on angle is the stronger one. A portfolio company in the same sector may be actively looking for exactly the size and region you are selling.

Read each firm's stated criteria before you reach out. Most publish a range for revenue or earnings, target sectors and preferred geographies. Contact both the deal team at the fund and, where it makes sense, the leadership of a relevant portfolio company. Note which you contacted so you never pitch the same deal to one firm twice from two directions without knowing it.

Independent sponsors and search funds

Independent sponsors are deal makers who find a company first and raise equity for that one deal. Search funds are usually led by one or two searchers backed by investors, looking for a single business to buy and run. Both groups are hunting for a specific kind of company, and both can move with real focus when a listing matches their thesis.

Ask about capital early. A sponsor should be able to explain who backs the deal and how equity will be raised. A searcher should be able to name the investors behind the search and whether the fund is still active. Our page on search fund deal sourcing explains how searchers look for companies, which helps you pitch them on their own terms.

Individual buyers

Individual buyers, sometimes called owner operators, want to buy a business and run it themselves. They are the main audience for many smaller listings, and they often come through listing sites and a broker's own database rather than direct outreach.

Individuals vary more than any other group. Some have deep industry experience and lender relationships already in place. Others are early in their search and still working out what they can afford. Screening matters most here, which is why proof of funds and a clear financing plan come up again later in this guide.

Score and tier the list

Once the research list is wide, score every name on three questions. Does the business fit what this buyer has bought or says it wants? Can the buyer fund a deal of this size? How likely is the buyer to act now rather than someday?

Group the scores into tiers. Tier one holds the best fit and most capable buyers, and they get the most personal approach. Tier two holds strong fits with a less certain mandate. Tier three is broader coverage that meets the basic criteria and gets a lighter touch.

Keep the research list and the release list separate. The research list is everything you found. The release list holds only the names the seller has approved and you have checked. Nobody is contacted from the research list.

Get the seller's sign-off on names

Walk the seller through the release list before the first message goes out. Sellers know things a database does not. They may know that one competitor has a habit of poaching staff, that a certain fund once made a lowball offer or that a supplier would treat news of a sale as leverage.

Pay the closest attention to direct competitors. Some sellers want them held back until other buyers are already engaged. Others are happy to approach them early under a strict NDA. Either way, record the decision and the reason next to each name so the whole team works from the same rules.

Treat silence as a no. If the seller has not approved a name, it stays off the release list until they do.

Write a blind teaser that protects the seller

The blind teaser, sometimes called a blind profile, is a one page summary that lets a buyer decide whether to ask for more without learning who the business is. It is the first document most buyers see, so it has to be clear and careful at the same time.

A good teaser usually covers the industry described in general terms, the region at the level of a state or a wider area, a size range for revenue or earnings, a few strengths such as customer mix or recurring work, the reason for the sale and what the seller wants from a buyer. It closes with one clear next step, which is to request the NDA.

Then read it as a competitor would. If a rival in the same town could guess the company from the details, widen the region, round the numbers or drop the detail. A teaser that is slightly vague is far better than one that exposes the seller.

Run the NDA flow

The NDA step is the gate between interest and information. Keep it simple for good buyers and firm for everyone else.

  • Screen the request. Before you send an NDA, ask a few short questions about who the buyer is, what they have bought and how they would fund a deal.
  • Send one standard NDA. Use the same form for every buyer, reviewed by the seller's attorney, with a clause covering employees and customers.
  • Log the signature. Record who signed, on behalf of which entity and on what date.
  • Release the memorandum. Only then share the name and the confidential information memorandum, ideally through a tracked data room.

If a buyer pushes back on the NDA or wants detail first, that tells you something. Serious buyers are used to signing them. A buyer who will not sign is usually not a buyer you want holding the seller's name.

Pick channels: email, LinkedIn and the phone

Most buyer outreach runs on three channels, and each plays a different role.

Email

Email carries the teaser and most of the follow-up. Keep the first note short. Say who you represent in general terms, why this buyer might care and what the next step is. Send from a domain tied to the brokerage and set it up properly so messages land in the inbox. Write each note to one person rather than a list.

LinkedIn

LinkedIn helps you confirm that a contact is the right person and still in the role. A connection request or a short message can also reach a decision maker who does not answer email. Keep the deal itself out of public posts and only share specifics in private messages.

Phone

A call is often the fastest way to reach a tier one buyer and the best way to qualify one. Many brokers call their top names personally and let email and LinkedIn cover the rest. When you call, have the teaser ready to send the moment the buyer says yes.

Set a follow-up cadence

Most buyers do not reply to the first note. That rarely means no. It usually means the message arrived on a busy day. A planned sequence of follow-ups catches the buyers who meant to answer and never did.

A simple pattern works for many listings. Send the first note, follow up a few business days later with one new detail, try a different channel after that and close the loop with a short last note that makes it easy to say no. Space the touches so the sequence feels attentive rather than pushy.

Launch your tiers close together where you can. If tier one hears about the deal weeks before tier three, the seller may end up negotiating with early buyers before later ones have even read the teaser. Keeping the waves tight helps buyers reach the same stage at roughly the same time.

Stop the moment a buyer asks you to stop. Respect opt outs on every channel, and remove the name from future listings unless the buyer says otherwise.

Qualify buyers and ask for proof of funds

Qualification protects the seller's time and the listing's confidentiality. Before a buyer meets the seller, you should know that the buyer can close and is likely to.

  • Proof of funds. A bank statement, a letter from a lender or a letter from the equity backers showing the buyer can cover the cash portion
  • Financing plan. How the rest is paid, whether through bank debt, seller financing, investor equity or a mix
  • Relevant experience. What the buyer has run or bought before and how that maps to this business
  • Decision process. Who signs off, whether investors must approve and what diligence the buyer expects
  • Fit with the seller's goals. Whether the buyer wants the owner to stay, how they treat staff and how they see the business growing

Ask for proof of funds at the right moment. Asking before the NDA can feel abrupt to a strategic buyer. Asking only after a seller meeting wastes the seller's time. For most listings, the point after the NDA and before the first seller meeting works well.

Track every buyer in one place

A buyer search touches many people across several weeks. Without one shared record, follow-ups slip, two people email the same buyer and nobody can tell the seller where things stand.

Use a CRM or a well kept spreadsheet with one row per buyer. Track the tier, the contact, every touch with its date and channel, the NDA status, when the memorandum was released, proof of funds, questions asked and the current stage. Add a notes field for anything the seller should know.

Review the tracker with the seller on a regular rhythm. A short update on how many buyers were contacted, how many asked for the NDA and who is moving forward keeps the seller calm and shows the work that is happening behind the scenes.

Confidentiality rules for the whole process

Confidentiality is not one step. It runs through the whole search. A few rules keep it intact.

  • Use a project code name in every note, file name and subject line.
  • Never put the company name, address or exact financials in a teaser.
  • Release the memorandum only after a signed NDA and a basic screen.
  • Track who opened which documents and remove access when a buyer drops out.
  • Keep the seller's employees, customers and suppliers out of the loop unless the seller decides otherwise.
  • Send to one named contact per firm and check the address before every send.

If a leak happens, tell the seller right away and agree on a response. Hiding a problem does far more damage than the leak itself.

Mistakes that stall a buyer search

  • Narrowing too early. Cutting the list before research is done leaves good buyers undiscovered.
  • Mass sending. One generic blast to a purchased list reads as spam and can expose the listing.
  • A teaser that identifies the business. Too much detail in the first touch defeats the point of going blind.
  • No follow-up. Stopping after one note misses most of the buyers who would have replied.
  • Slow memorandum release. A signed NDA followed by silence cools a warm buyer quickly.
  • Skipping qualification. Unscreened buyers take the seller's time and see private numbers they should not.
  • Scattered records. Notes spread across inboxes make it hard to know who said what.

Run it in house or outsource it

Many brokers run buyer outreach themselves, and for a listing with a strong existing buyer file that can be enough. The work gets heavy when several listings are live at once, when the right buyers sit outside the broker's usual network or when nobody on the team has hours each week for research and follow-up.

An outsourced partner takes on the research, the writing and the follow-up while the broker keeps the relationship, the advice and the deal itself. The broker still approves the buyer list and every message. The partner simply makes sure the outreach happens consistently on every listing, not only on the one that is closest to closing.

What to ask an outsourced buyer outreach partner

Whoever you hire, these questions separate a careful partner from a list vendor.

  • Do you build a buyer list for each listing, or send from a standing list?
  • Which buyer types do you research, and how do you check each contact?
  • Do messages go out in our firm's name, and do we approve each one first?
  • What stays out of the first touch, and who sends the NDA?
  • How do you screen replies before they reach us?
  • When the engagement ends, does the buyer file stay with us?
  • What do you leave to us, and where does your role stop?

Clear, specific answers to these questions are a good sign. Vague answers about volume or bold claims about how many buyers will reply are a reason to keep looking.

Frequently asked questions

What is sell-side buyer outreach for business brokers?

It is the work of finding and contacting likely buyers for a business a broker has listed. The broker builds a buyer list, sends a blind teaser, signs NDAs with interested buyers, follows up and qualifies each buyer before introducing the serious ones to the seller.

Which buyers should a broker contact for a listing?

Most listings draw from strategic acquirers, private equity groups and their portfolio companies, independent sponsors, search funds and individual buyers. The right mix depends on the size of the business, the industry and what the seller wants from a buyer.

What goes in a blind teaser?

A general description of the industry, the region at a broad level, a size range, a few strengths, the reason for the sale and a clear next step. It leaves out the company name, the address and anything else that could identify the business.

When should a buyer sign the NDA?

Once the buyer has read the blind teaser, asked for more and passed a short screen. The NDA comes before the broker shares the company name or the confidential information memorandum.

How do brokers check proof of funds?

Brokers usually ask for a bank statement, a lender letter or a letter from equity backers that shows the buyer can cover the cash portion of a deal. Most ask after the NDA and before the buyer meets the seller.

How many times should a broker follow up with a buyer?

Several planned touches across email, LinkedIn and the phone work better than a single note. Space them out, add something new each time and stop at once if the buyer asks you to.

Can a broker outsource buyer outreach?

Yes. Some brokers hand the research, outreach and follow-up to an outsourced partner such as Be Leaded and keep the approvals and the deal itself in house.

Does Be Leaded advise on the deal?

No. Be Leaded handles research, outreach and scheduling with interested buyers. The broker's team handles valuation, negotiation and the deal itself.
