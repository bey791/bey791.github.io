Facebook and Instagram ads for dental practices

Be Leaded plans Meta campaigns on Facebook and Instagram for dental offices seeking more people actually sitting in a chair. Work is split by treatment type. Each inquiry goes to your front desk. Booked consults and seated visits, as your office records them, are measures only, never promises.

Dental practices use this program to run Facebook and Instagram campaigns without building the ads in house. We produce the creative, set up the lead form or a short page and send each inquiry to your front desk. Your practice owns the ad account, and a dentist signs off on every ad before launch. Your front desk makes the first contact. We never contact patients. Booked consults and seated visits, as your office records them, are measures only, never promises.

Book a 30-minute call

30 minutes: your offices, treatment types and how your front desk books consults. No pitch deck.

What the program includes

Your practice owns the ad account, picks the consult types and decides when a visit counts as booked or seated. A dentist at the practice signs off on every ad before anything is submitted to Meta. Ads also have to follow the practice's state dental board rules. Be Leaded is a marketing vendor, not a clinic, and we offer no clinical advice.

  • Creative built around the consult types you already offer, including new-patient exams, aligner evaluations and implant or full-arch consults
  • Either a Meta lead form or a short page, one offer at a time, with one scheduling question only
  • A geographic radius around each office, with an 18-or-older audience when the offer counts as a cosmetic procedure
  • Routing into your front desk or practice software, with alerts and visit reminders inside the tools you already use, and missed-call text back that follows the practice's own consent rules
  • A record of booked consults and seated visits, taken from what your office reports, as measures only, never as promises
  • A weekly look at what ran, what should be paused and the next test to try

After the call we settle the program scope and our fee. Your practice pays Meta directly for media spend, which sits outside our fee.

Campaigns by treatment type

New-patient exams, cosmetic or aligner consults and implant or full-arch consults each get their own campaign, with their own creative, form and measures, because those offers are not interchangeable. Audience setup uses the area around each office and the offer on the ad. The copy names a service the practice already provides. It does not address the viewer's teeth, pain or appearance.

Offers we can run by consult type

New-patient exams are often the first offer a practice wants to advertise, because they are a visit the office already provides and the front desk already knows how to book. The ad names that exam or a new-patient visit. It does not tell the viewer they have a dental problem, and it does not invent a discount the practice does not already offer under its board rules.

Aligner evaluations get their own campaign and their own form, because the person who wants that consult is not interchangeable with someone booking a routine exam. The ad can name aligners or, when it matches the offer the practice already provides, a brand the practice uses, without implying a partnership we have not confirmed. Implant and full-arch consults are set up the same way: a separate campaign, a consult the practice actually offers and no promise of a finished result in the copy.

Emergency visits are only added when the practice can truly see an urgent case in a time frame the front desk will stand behind. The routing has to reach someone who can book that visit. The copy still names the service. It does not say the viewer is in pain or has an injury.

Returning-patient outreach, when the practice wants it, is planned from a list the office already keeps of patients who have agreed the practice may get in touch, and it is described in the section below. Each of these offers is measured on booked consults and seated visits for that offer alone, as the office records them, and those figures are measures only, never promises.

A whitening visit or a short seasonal offer is used only when the practice already provides that visit and the board rules allow the way it is described. It gets its own campaign rather than being folded into a new-patient or cosmetic consult. We do not invent a promotion to make an ad more eye-catching, and we do not run two consult types in one ad just to fill a calendar.

Ad creative we build by treatment

Creative is written for one consult type at a time, and a dentist at the practice signs off on every asset before it is submitted to Meta. Each piece names a visit the office already offers and does not attach a result, a testimonial or a price.

An implant consult explainer video walks through what happens at the consult. It can show the office, introduce the dentist who will run the visit and describe how a person books. It does not show a finished implant case as a promised result, and it does not imply that the person watching already needs an implant.

Aligner ads often raise a request for before and after style images. State dental boards and Meta each have rules that can limit those images, including how a result may be shown, whether a disclaimer is required and whether a patient likeness may appear at all. Board rules are not the same in every state, and Meta's advertising standards can change. This page is not legal advice. Your practice should check its own state dental board and Meta's current policies before deciding what is allowed. On this program we do not use before-and-after photos or testimonials. An aligner ad instead uses a still or a short clip that names the evaluation and points to a consult the practice already provides.

A new-patient exam offer card is a still ad that names the exam or a new-patient visit, shows the practice name and contact details and invites the person to book. It does not print a fee, a discount or a special price, and it does not invent a promotion the practice does not already offer under its board rules.

An emergency visit ad is used only when the practice already offers visits for urgent pain or a broken tooth. The ad names that urgent visit as a service. It does not say the viewer is in pain or has a broken tooth, and it does not promise a visit on the same day or in the same week.

A reactivation message is written for past patients who have already agreed the practice may get in touch. It can invite them back for a visit they have received before, such as a hygiene appointment, or for a consult that was never seated. The message names a visit, does not include a testimonial and still needs a dentist's sign-off before it is used.

From inquiry to a seated visit

After a form is submitted, your front desk gets a text and email alert. We configure delivery into your practice software and add appointment reminders in the tools you already use, plus missed-call text back that follows the practice's own consent rules. Your front desk makes the first contact, places the consult and records whether the person attends. We never contact patients. This page does not name a reply-time target, because attendance depends on how quickly your office reaches each person.

The stages we watch are inquiry, first contact, a booked consult and a seated visit. How fast the first contact happens is the main lever on that path. Those stages are logged from what your office reports and are measures only, never promises.

How to read cost per booked consult and per seated visit

A form fill is not the number that tells you whether the spend is working. The useful readings are what the practice spent, across the Meta bill and what we charge, for every consult the front desk actually booked, and then for each of those consults that turned into a seated visit. The seated figure is the one that reflects a person in the chair. A low cost per inquiry can still be a poor result if few of those inquiries become booked or seated visits.

On the weekly review we sit with the counts your office recorded for each offer and each office, and we look at that path rather than at a raw pile of forms. We do not publish dollar amounts, ratios or figures from other practices on this page, and we do not treat a busy form as a reason to keep a campaign that is not producing seated visits. If one offer is booking and seating visits and another is not, we change or pause the weaker one. Those readings stay measures, not promises of what the next month will bring.

Bring the practice's own counts to that review: what was spent, how many inquiries arrived, how many were booked and how many were seated, for each offer. Media billed by Meta and our fee should be kept apart, then both should be read against seated visits, not against clicks. A figure from another office, or from a published benchmark, is not a substitute for those notes, and this page does not provide one. If the office cannot yet tell us which inquiries were seated, we cannot read cost per seated visit and we say so rather than guess.

A lead form or a landing page

A Meta lead form is shorter and stays inside the ad. A landing page can explain the consult in more detail before the person submits. We pick one per campaign with you on the call.

The form asks for contact details and one scheduling question only, such as which office or which consult type they want to book. It does not ask about symptoms, medical history or insurance. Clinical questions stay with your practice after the person is in touch.

Returning visitors and returning patients

If someone sees an ad, visits a page or starts a form and does not book, we can later show a further ad to people who did that on a property the practice controls, once the practice has approved the notice and the tag. The later ad still names a consult the office offers. It does not say the person needs treatment, is in pain or has a condition.

Separately, a practice may want to invite past patients back for a visit they already received, such as a hygiene appointment or a consult that was never seated. That work starts from a list the office already keeps of patients who have agreed the practice may get in touch. We do not build it from a health condition, a treatment history we infer or a purchased list. The practice confirms that the list may be used this way. Creative for that list still goes through a dentist at the practice before it is submitted.

Both kinds of follow-up stay under the same copy rules as the first ad. We pause them when the front desk says the volume is already more than it can reach, or when the notes show that people are booking from the first ad and do not need another reminder. We do not keep a person in a follow-up audience because of a condition we think they have, and we do not buy a list of dental patients to start this work.

Practices with more than one office

When a practice has more than one location, each office needs its own radius, its own booking path and a clear rule for which desk receives the inquiry. Creative can name the office the person would actually visit. We do not send every response to a single shared inbox if the practice wants each office to handle its own callers, and we do not mix two offices in one campaign when their offers or hours differ.

Dental groups and DSOs can use the same structure, with one person named who signs off on ads and a front desk path that still reaches the office where the visit will happen. We do not treat a group as a different kind of buyer, and we do not promise a volume of visits across a chain. The work still starts with one offer and one area, then adds offices only when the desks can keep up with the inquiries they are already receiving.

If offices share a brand but have different dentists, hours or consult types, those differences are written into the ads and into the booking path rather than hidden. The weekly review is split by office as well as by offer, so a strong location is not used to hide a weak one. We do not claim a special method for groups, and we do not turn this page into a promise that a chain of offices will fill at the same pace.

What we review together each week

The weekly review is a short scorecard, not a slide deck of vanity totals. For each active offer and each office we look at how many inquiries arrived, how quickly the front desk made first contact, how many consults were booked and how many of those consults were seated, as your office recorded them. We also look at which ads were paused and which test should run next.

If first contact is slow, that is usually the first thing to fix, because a late call often means a consult that never gets booked. If one offer is seating visits and another is not, we move attention to the one that is working or we change the weaker creative, form or area. If the desk is already full, we do not add a new offer until the practice is ready to handle it. None of those readings is a promise about next week, and this page does not attach a number or a rate to any of them.

The practice should come to the review with those counts already in hand, or with a way for us to read them from the software the desk already uses. We do not score the week on likes, comments or a cheap click. If a booked consult or a seated visit was not recorded, it does not appear on the scorecard, and we would rather leave a cell blank than invent a total. The scorecard is a way to decide the next change, not a report card we publish.

The ethics and ad-policy rules we follow

Nothing here is clinical or legal advice. A dentist at the practice signs off on every ad. Ads also have to follow the practice's state dental board rules.

Rules that apply to the practice

  • A dentist at the practice signs off on every ad, form and landing page before anything is submitted to Meta.
  • Ads also have to follow the practice's state dental board rules.
  • Campaigns are written to sit inside Meta's advertising policies, the ADA Principles of Ethics and Code of Professional Conduct, and the advertising rules of your state dental board. Your practice confirms which of those rules apply to your offices.
  • Board rules are not the same in every state. Many boards limit how specialties, fees, discounts, testimonials and promises about outcomes may be described. Creative is planned around the limits your practice identifies.
  • Booked consults and seated visits are measures only, never promises.
  • We do not use before-and-after photos or testimonials.
  • The practice name and contact details appear on every ad.
  • Be Leaded is a marketing vendor, not a clinic and not a patient referral service. We offer no clinical or legal advice.
  • Section 5.F of the ADA Code (advertising) and sections 5.H and 5.I (specialty and general-practice announcements) are a useful reference. The binding rules are the ones issued by your state dental board.

Rules Meta applies to the ads

  • Dental services are not one of Meta's Special Ad Categories. Every dental ad still has to meet Meta's advertising standards, and Meta may refuse or take down an ad.
  • Meta forbids ad copy that implies the advertiser already knows a personal fact about the viewer, including a medical or dental condition. Copy on this program names the service on offer. It does not address the viewer's mouth, pain or looks.
  • Ads for cosmetic procedures are limited to people 18 or older under Meta's Health and Wellness standard. Meta lists dental products such as teeth whitening products as an exception to that age floor. When an offer is a cosmetic procedure, we apply the adult setting. Your practice and Meta's review decide how a given offer is classified.
  • The same Health and Wellness standard bars copy that attacks a person's appearance or hygiene, and it bars a promise of a set result in a set time without a qualifier in a health or cosmetic context.
  • Lead forms are tightly limited. Ours collect contact details and one scheduling question only. Symptoms, medical history and insurance are left to your practice.
  • Clinical detail stays out of audiences, pixels and form fields. Campaigns are written to sit inside Meta's advertising policies. Approval by Meta is never guaranteed.

Buyers who want Meta and search work for a business-to-business pipeline, not local patient consults, can see our Meta and search programs for B2B sellers.

What Meta and HIPAA restrict

Two different sets of limits shape this work, and they should not be collapsed into one. Meta's advertising standards, including its personal attributes rule and its Health and Wellness standard, already linked in the sources below, restrict how an ad may speak to the person who sees it, who may be shown an ad for a cosmetic procedure and what a lead form may ask. That is why copy on this program names a service and does not address the viewer's mouth, pain or looks. It is also why forms stay limited to contact details and one scheduling question, and why treatment details stay out of audiences, pixels and event fields.

HIPAA is a federal rule about protected health information, and it is aimed at health care providers and their vendors, not at a social ad network's public ad policy. Whether a given list, reminder or intake record is subject to HIPAA is a question for the practice and its counsel, not a finding we make on this page. The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services publishes the current HIPAA privacy rule pages. Our part of the process is to keep clinical information out of the ad, the form, the audience and the event, and to send inquiries only to the practice. We do not give HIPAA advice, and we do not describe Meta approval as a substitute for the practice's own privacy review.

Questions about business associate agreements, how long a record is kept or who inside the practice may see an inquiry are for the practice and its counsel. We will follow the process the practice sets for how inquiries are delivered and who can access the ad account. We do not use a patient list, a form response or a seated-visit note for any other practice.

Questions

Short answers about running Meta campaigns for a dental office.

Do Facebook ads work for dentists?

They can produce inquiries from people who are looking for a dental office, provided the desk can respond on the day the form arrives. Markets, offers and response speed all change the outcome. We score the work on booked consults and seated visits as your office records them. Those figures are measures only, never promises.

How should a practice measure the ads?

Score the path from inquiry to first contact, then to a booked consult and a seated visit. Those stages are logged from what your office reports and are measures only, never promises.

Can you run ads for implants, aligners and new-patient exams?

Yes. Separate campaigns cover the consult types you already offer, including new-patient exams, aligner evaluations and implant or full-arch consults. Invisalign can appear in the ad when it matches the offer, without implying a brand partnership.

Do the ads say the viewer needs dental treatment?

No. Meta forbids ad copy that implies the advertiser already knows a personal fact about the viewer, including a medical or dental condition. Copy on this program names the service on offer. It does not address the viewer's mouth, pain or looks.

What can the lead form ask?

The form collects contact details and one scheduling question only, such as which office or which consult type they want to book. It does not ask about symptoms, medical history or insurance. Clinical questions stay with your practice after the person is in touch.

Who holds the Meta account and how do fees work?

Who approves treatment claims in the ads?
