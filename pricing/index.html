What the monthly fee includes

The fee pays for the work that turns a market definition into booked conversations. That work has several parts, and each one is a place where a cheaper quote can quietly leave something out. Here is each part in plain terms.

List building and verification

We match accounts and contacts to the buyer you describe and verify the addresses before the first send. Before the list goes into the program, a sample comes to you to check. A file of names with nobody answering behind it is not the service.

Sending domains, inboxes and warmup

Cold email goes out from dedicated sending domains built for the program. They carry SPF, DKIM and DMARC. The inboxes are warmed before real prospecting starts. The domain on your business cards never sends the cold mail. The technical background is on cold email deliverability.

Sequences written for your market

The copy is written for the buyer and the problem you sell against, and nothing sends until you approve it. Plain-text sequences are the default because they read like mail from a person rather than a campaign.

LinkedIn alongside email

LinkedIn outreach runs in step with the email, from your team's own profiles. One buyer can be reached on both channels, and once someone replies on one channel the other goes quiet.

Reply handling and booking

Replies are read and answered, and a buyer who matches the definition you approved is booked on your calendar. A polite reply that misses the definition is not turned into a meeting to make a week look full.

Weekly reporting

Each week's report sums up what was sent, who replied and which meetings were set, then says what will change. Every reply thread is open to you, so the report is a summary of something you can check.

The contact target

Target up to 25,000 new contacts a month.

Tools, data and infrastructure sit inside the fee. You do not buy a sequencer, a data subscription or inboxes on top of it. If a quote you are comparing lists any of those as extras, add them back before you compare the totals.

What is billed at cost

Two things sit outside the fee and are paid at cost: ad spend and call minutes. They are separate because they belong to other companies. Both are billed at cost, so you can see what each one is.

Ad spend

If a program includes paid social or search, the ad budget is paid to Meta, Google or LinkedIn directly. It is not part of our fee. The fee covers the work around the ads. The money you set aside for the platforms is a separate line that you control and can change.

Call minutes

Voice programs add call minutes at cost. Calling is quoted separately from the email and LinkedIn program, and the cold calling services page explains how phone outreach runs beside the other two channels.

Why this split helps you

A single number that hides ad spend or minutes makes quotes hard to compare. Keeping them apart means a larger ad budget never looks like a larger agency fee, and a program with no calling never pays for it.

What changes the price

Four things, and we'll walk through each of them on the call. None of them is a surcharge. Each one describes how much work the program contains.

Market size and volume

How many accounts fit your buyer, and how many inboxes it takes to reach them at a safe pace.

A narrow market, such as one role at one kind of company in one region, is a short list worked with care. A wide market is a long list that needs more sending capacity spread across more domains. The extra capacity is part of the work, so it is part of the number.

Volume also depends on how fast the list can be reached without hurting delivery. Pushing harder through the same inboxes is the wrong fix. Adding inboxes and domains is the right one, and that is what moves the fee. The outbound calculator turns a contact target into mailboxes and domains if you want a rough picture before the call.

Channels

Email alone, or email with LinkedIn, paid social and search, newsletters, SMS or voice. Ad spend and call minutes are paid at cost, on top of the fee.

Each channel you add is a separate piece of work with its own setup, its own copy and its own replies to read. Email on its own is the lightest scope. Email with LinkedIn is a wider one. Paid social and search, newsletters, SMS and voice are programs in their own right, and when you run one beside email the fee reflects both.

Starting with one channel is normal. Many buyers begin with email and add another once the first one is running and the replies have shown what the market responds to.

Offers and segments

One offer to one buyer, or several offers to different segments, each with its own list and copy.

Think of a segment as a small program inside the program. A software vendor selling to finance leads and to IT leads has two lists, two sets of sequences and two sets of replies to handle. The problem a finance lead cares about is not the one an IT lead cares about, so the message cannot be shared.

That is why segment count is one of the biggest levers on the fee. A second segment needs its own list and its own copy, so it adds work. If you are unsure how many segments you need, start with the one that is easiest to describe and add the next once you see replies.

Replies and booking

Whether we handle every reply and book meetings, or hand warm replies to your team.

Handling replies is real work. An interested reply needs an answer, a time and a calendar hold. A question needs a plain response. A wrong person needs one polite ask for the right one. A no needs to stop the sequence at once.

If your team prefers to take warm replies and book its own meetings, the work on our side is smaller and the number reflects that. If you want every reply handled and meetings placed on the calendar for you, the work is larger. Neither choice is better. It depends on how much of your team's week you want the replies to take.

How agencies price: retainer, pay per meeting and hybrid

Most agency quotes fall into one of three shapes. The shape tells you who carries the risk and what you are really buying, so it is worth knowing before you compare any numbers.

Retainer

A retainer is a fixed monthly fee for a defined program. You pay the same amount whether the month is busy or quiet. What you are buying is the work itself: the list, the sending, the writing, the answers to replies and the booked time. The strength is predictability. The trade-off is that the fee does not depend on how many meetings a month produces.

Pay per meeting

Pay per meeting bills when a meeting is booked or held. The attraction is obvious, because you pay for an outcome. The catch is that the word meeting has to be defined in writing. Booked, held and qualified are three different things, and a loose definition can make a per meeting fee look cheaper than it is.

Hybrid

A hybrid uses a smaller monthly base plus a fee per meeting. It splits the risk: the base pays for the work and the per meeting fee pays for results. The trade-off is that two prices need two sets of terms, so read both the base and the meeting definition before you compare it with a plain retainer.

Who carries the risk when a month is quiet

Every pricing shape puts the risk of a slow month somewhere. Knowing where it sits is as useful as knowing the fee.

On a retainer, you carry it. The work happens and is billed whatever the replies look like, because markets are uneven and some months are slower than others. The report is how you see whether the work is sound and whether the targeting needs to change.

On pay per meeting, the agency carries more of it. That often shows up in how tightly the agency defines a meeting, in how many accounts it is willing to take on and in how fast it wants to move. An agency paid only on meetings has a reason to book the easy ones.

On a hybrid the risk is shared, and the split depends on how large the base is. A small base with a large per meeting fee behaves like pay per meeting. A large base with a small per meeting fee behaves like a retainer.

None of these is the wrong choice. The mistake is choosing one without asking who pays when the market is slow. Put the question to every firm you talk to.

Booked, held and qualified: what a meeting means in a quote

The word meeting hides three separate events, and quotes that look alike can price different ones.

  1. Booked. A time is on a calendar. It says nothing yet about whether anyone turns up.
  2. Held. The buyer attended. This is the first point where the conversation exists.
  3. Qualified. The buyer matched a definition you wrote down, such as the role, the type of account and the problem.

A price per booked meeting depends on a time being on the calendar. A price per held meeting depends on the buyer attending. A price per qualified meeting depends on a definition, so the definition matters as much as the fee.

If you are comparing a per meeting quote with a monthly program, ask the firm to write down which of the three it bills and what happens when a buyer does not show. Ask who decides whether a meeting meets the definition. If that person is the one being paid, you want the rule written before the first invoice.

On a monthly program the question comes out differently. We write the definition with you before outreach starts, and a meeting is booked when a reply matches it. The definition keeps the calendar honest even though the fee does not depend on the count.

Setup fees, minimum terms and notice

Three contract details change what a quote costs over a year, and none of them is visible in the monthly figure.

Setup fees

Some firms charge a one time fee to build the sending setup and the first lists. That can be reasonable if it is stated, and it should be part of your total when you compare. Ask for it as a line, with what it covers.

Minimum terms

A minimum term is the number of months you commit to at signature. A longer commitment lowers the vendor's risk and raises yours. Add the whole commitment up, not only the monthly fee. A quote that looks close on a monthly basis can look very different once the minimum term is multiplied through.

Notice

Notice is how long before you leave you have to say so. A short notice window gives you room to change course. A long one can tie you in after the term ends. Ask what it is and where it is written.

How to compare two quotes

Two quotes only compare when they describe the same work. Here is a plain way to put them side by side.

  1. Turn each quote into one monthly figure. Include the fee and any setup cost spread across the term. Add data, domains, inboxes and tools. Add every channel you want.
  2. Write down what is committed at signature. Note the setup fee, the minimum term and the notice period.
  3. Check how a meeting is defined. Booked, held and qualified are not interchangeable.
  4. List who does each part. Check who builds the list, writes the copy, reads the replies and books the time.
  5. Ask what you keep. Find out who owns the lists, the replies and the sending domains if you leave.

Here is an example with invented labels and no figures. Quote A lists a lower monthly fee, with data billed per record and inboxes billed as an add-on. Quote B lists a higher monthly fee with data, inboxes and tools inside. After step one, the gap between them narrows. After step two, a long minimum term on Quote A can reverse it. Neither conclusion shows in the headline fee.

Do not turn a cost per lead into a cost per meeting by guessing at conversion. Any ratio you invent will be an assumption rather than a fact. Ask each firm what they bill and for what, then compare like with like.

What a cheap quote can leave out

A low number is not a problem. A low number that leaves out half the work is. These are the usual gaps, and each one can be asked about on a call.

  • Data. The list is the foundation. If it is billed per record or sold as a separate package, the monthly figure is not the real cost.
  • Sending infrastructure. Domains, inboxes and warmup have to exist before the first send, and someone has to maintain them.
  • Tools. Sequencers, enrichment and verification all cost money. They are either in the fee or on your bill.
  • Replies. If the quote covers sending only, someone on your team has to read, answer and book. That person's time is a cost.
  • Channels. A fee that covers email only is not comparable with one that covers email and LinkedIn.
  • Reporting. A program you cannot read is a program you cannot judge.

If any of these is missing, ask what it costs and who does it. The answer often turns a cheap quote into an ordinary one.

Doing it yourself with tools and data

A third option is to buy the tools and run outbound yourself. In our view the trade is cash against your team's time.

The work behind a self-run program has the same shape as the fee on this page. Someone has to define the buyer, find accounts and contacts, verify the addresses, register domains, set up and warm inboxes, write sequences, send at a safe pace, read replies and book the time. Each of those has a subscription, a task or both.

  1. Pick the tools: a data source, a verifier, a sequencer and a way to manage inboxes.
  2. Build the sending setup with the right authentication, then warm it before real sending.
  3. Build and clean the list, and keep it clean as people change jobs.
  4. Write the copy for each segment and test it.
  5. Monitor delivery and fix problems quickly.
  6. Read every reply and book every meeting.

That list is the work someone on your team would own. The subscriptions are the visible cost. The time is the hidden one, so count it before you decide. If a person on your team has the time and wants to learn the craft, doing it yourself is reasonable. If not, the fee on this page is paying for that time as well as the tools.

Which option fits your situation

There is no single right answer, and anyone who tells you there is has a quote to sell. Here is how the three routes tend to match different situations.

Hiring fits when

You have a sales manager with time to coach, a product that takes real learning and a plan that justifies a long ramp. You want one person to own the conversation from the first email to the close.

An agency program fits when

You want outbound running without a hire, you have a buyer you can describe and you are ready for your sellers to take meetings that arrive on the calendar. You also want to see the work as it happens rather than as a quarterly summary.

Doing it yourself fits when

You have someone on the team who will run the program every week. That person is comfortable with deliverability and data work. The market is small enough to work by hand.

A mix is common

Many teams hire a rep for live conversations and use a program for the outreach that fills the calendar. The rep gets meetings to run instead of a list to build. Both pay for different work, so the two do not overlap.

What a first month looks like

Pricing makes more sense once you know what the work looks like. This is the order it follows.

  1. Kickoff. It comes after the first month's payment. We lock the buyer definition, the meeting definition and the calendar that receives the bookings.
  2. Build. We build and verify the list. We register and warm the sending domains and mailboxes. We write copy for you to approve.
  3. Go live. Sending begins when warmup is finished and you have approved the copy. Most programs go live in about three weeks.
  4. Run. We send the mail, read what comes back and place qualified meetings on your calendar. A weekly review looks at what is working.
  5. Stay or cancel. You decide after each month whether to continue.

The first weeks are mostly build and approval, so there is little to see until sending starts. After that, how quickly replies turn into meetings depends on your market and your offer. Some markets answer quickly and others take longer, and the weekly report shows where yours sits.

What you approve before anything sends

A fee is easier to trust when you control what it produces. Three approvals sit with you before outreach begins.

  • A sample of the list. You check that the accounts and contacts match the buyer you described.
  • The copy. Every sequence is yours to read and change before it is sent.
  • The calendar. You choose whose calendar receives the booking, so meetings land with the person who will take them.

Sending waits until the targets and the buyer rules are agreed. If the replies later show that the definition needs to change, the weekly review is where it changes. This is also why the fee is a program rather than a unit price. The work includes the adjustments.

Who owns the lists, replies and domains

Ownership is a pricing question because it decides what you are left with when the contract ends.

The contact file and every reply thread stay yours. We manage the sending domains and mailboxes we build for the program while you are a client. What happens to them if you stop is a fair question for the call, because that is the part that varies most between firms.

Whichever firm you choose, ask four things in writing. Who owns the contact list? Who owns the reply threads? Who holds the registrar account for the sending domains? What is delivered on the last day? A firm with clear answers has usually thought about the end of the relationship, which is a good sign about the rest.

What a weekly report should show

A program you cannot read is one you cannot judge. A useful report is short and answers four questions.

  • Who was contacted this week, and on which channels?
  • What did they say?
  • Which conversations matched the definition you approved?
  • What is changing next, and why?

Ours follows that shape: sends, replies, meetings and the next change, with every reply thread open to you. When you compare quotes, ask each firm to show a sample report. If the report is only a count of emails sent, you are not seeing the work.

When a monthly program is not the right fit

A monthly program is not right for everyone, and it is better to know that before a call.

How to tell whether a program is worth the fee

The right time to judge a program is while it is running, using what the reports show, and not only on the day a contract ends.

  • Look at who is being contacted. The accounts should match the buyer you described. If they do not, the targeting needs to change.
  • Read the replies yourself. A thread tells you more than a count. Look for real interest and for the kind of objection the market is raising.
  • Check the meetings against your definition. A calendar that fills with buyers who do not fit is a warning, not a result.
  • Watch what changes. A good program adjusts its list, its copy or its targeting as the replies come in.

Because the term is month to month, you can act on what you see. The fee is worth it when the work is sound and the conversations are the ones you wanted, and your own judgment decides that.

Common mistakes when comparing agency prices

Most bad decisions on agency pricing come from the same few habits.

  • Comparing the headline fee. A monthly number says nothing about data, inboxes, tools or channels.
  • Ignoring the minimum term. A short monthly price with a long commitment can cost more than it looks.
  • Treating a lead as a meeting. A positive reply is not a held conversation with a qualified buyer.
  • Forgetting the reply work. If your team has to read, answer and book, that time belongs in the comparison.
  • Skipping ownership. The list, the threads and the sending domains are worth asking about before you sign.
  • Leaving out ad spend and call minutes. They sit outside the fee, so add them to the total if you run those channels.

Avoiding these is mostly a matter of asking more questions before you decide. The list in the section above is a good place to start.

Questions about pricing

Why don't you publish one fixed price?

Because the work depends on your market. Reaching 800 accounts in one niche is a different program from reaching 25,000 across several segments. We'd rather give you an exact number after a 30-minute call than a price that doesn't fit.
